### I. The Absurd

- [ ] **The Absurd: A Confrontation, Not a Property of the World**
      — The absurd is the clash between our need for meaning and the universe's silent indifference to that need.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1, §3.1
- [ ] **The Absurd Is Not Nihilism**
      — Recognizing the absurd does not mean concluding that nothing matters; Camus explicitly rejects that nihilistic leap.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1, §3.1
- [ ] **Hope as a Hidden Trap**
      — Camus argues that hope, especially hope in an afterlife or ultimate reward, is a way of fleeing from real life.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2
- [ ] **Living Without God: Camus's Starting Point**
      — Camus's philosophy begins from the plain assumption that there is no God and no life after death, without arguing for it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2
- [ ] **The World's Silence**
      — Camus describes the physical universe as indifferent and "silent," neither answering nor caring about human demands for purpose.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1
- [ ] **A Feeling Before It's an Argument**
      — Camus treats the "sense of the absurd" as a widespread mood people already feel, not a conclusion he first has to prove.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1
- [ ] **Why Camus Distrusts Big Philosophical Systems**
      — Camus is deeply skeptical of grand rational systems that claim to fully explain existence, calling that ambition dishonest.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2
### II. The Three Responses Camus Rejects

- [ ] **Physical Suicide: Escaping the Question, Not Answering It**
      — Camus rejects suicide as a response to the absurd because it destroys the person facing the problem instead of resolving it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1; IEP "Suicide"
- [ ] **Philosophical Suicide: The Leap That Explains the Problem Away**
      — Camus calls it "philosophical suicide" when a thinker resolves the absurd by leaping to religious faith or a hidden cosmic order.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1, §3.3
- [ ] **Kierkegaard's Leap of Faith, According to Camus**
      — Camus argues Kierkegaard correctly diagnosed the absurd but then betrayed it by leaping to religious faith to escape it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.3
- [ ] **Husserl's Turn, According to Camus**
      — Camus praises Husserl's early method of careful description but says his later search for fixed essences repeats the same evasion.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.3
- [ ] **Why Camus Treats Faith and Idealist Philosophy as the Same Move**
      — Camus groups religious belief and certain rationalist philosophies together because both quietly promise an order the absurd denies exists.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1, §3.3
- [ ] **The Third Way: Living Without Appeal**
      — Camus's own answer is neither suicide nor faith, but staying alive while refusing every comforting explanation of the world.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2
### III. Revolt

- [ ] **Revolt: Saying "No" and "Yes" at the Same Time**
      — Camus defines revolt as refusing an intolerable condition while simultaneously affirming that some things are worth protecting.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1; IEP "Revolt"
- [ ] **"I Revolt, Therefore We Are"**
      — Camus reframes Descartes's "I think, therefore I am" into "I revolt, therefore we are," making rebellion inherently social.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1
- [ ] **Revolt Implies a Shared Human Nature**
      — Camus argues that the very act of rebelling assumes there is a common human nature worth defending in everyone.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Revolt"
- [ ] **Revolt Is Not the Same as Selfishness**
      — Although revolt starts with one person's refusal, Camus insists it is not egoism because it defends a good bigger than the self.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Revolt"
- [ ] **Revolt Has Built-In Limits**
      — Because revolt defends a shared human dignity, Camus argues it cannot logically justify destroying that same dignity in others.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1, §4.3
- [ ] **From One Person's Refusal to Solidarity With Everyone**
      — Camus holds that authentic revolt naturally expands outward from personal refusal into solidarity with all who suffer similar injustice.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1
- [ ] **Revolt Answers Both of Camus's Big Questions**
      — Camus treats revolt as his answer both to "why not kill myself" and, later, to "why not kill others."
      — _Source:_ SEP §1, §4.1
### IV. Rebellion vs. Revolution

- [ ] **Rebellion vs. Revolution: A Crucial Distinction**
      — Camus sharply distinguishes personal, limited rebellion from organized revolution, which he thinks tends to betray rebellion's original spirit.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4; IEP "Revolt"
- [ ] **The Execution of Louis XVI as a Turning Point**
      — Camus treats the French Revolution's execution of the king as a symbolic moment when rebellion first claimed unlimited authority.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2
- [ ] **How Revolt Curdles Into Nihilistic Violence**
      — Camus argues that revolt turns nihilistic once it stops defending human dignity and starts justifying killing for an abstract future goal.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2, §4.3
- [ ] **Communism as a Kind of Substitute Religion**
      — Camus argues that revolutionary Marxism replaces belief in God with belief in History, but keeps the same structure of blind faith.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2
- [ ] **"Logical Crime": Murder Justified by Theory**
      — Camus coins the idea of "logical crime" for mass killing that a political doctrine presents as rationally necessary rather than as a crime.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2
- [ ] **Camus's Critique of "Historical Necessity"**
      — Camus rejects the idea that appeals to historical necessity or inevitable progress can excuse present violence and injustice.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2
- [ ] **Violence: Both Inevitable and Impossible to Justify**
      — Camus admits that avoiding all violence may be impossible in an unjust world, yet insists no violence is ever simply justified.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3
### V. Camus, Sartre, and the "Existentialist" Label

- [ ] **Camus Rejected the Label "Existentialist"**
      — Despite being widely grouped with Sartre, Camus repeatedly and explicitly denied that he was an existentialist.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Existentialism"
- [ ] **Why "Existentialist" Doesn't Fit: No System, No Ontology**
      — Camus refused the existentialist label partly because he distrusted total philosophical systems and avoided deep questions about the structure of being.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Existentialism"
- [ ] **Camus vs. Sartre on Human Nature**
      — Unlike Sartre's "existence precedes essence," Camus believed there is a shared human nature with inherent dignity, closer to older humanist views.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Revolt", "Existentialism"
- [ ] **The Rebel and the Public Break With Sartre**
      — The 1951 publication of The Rebel, and its attack on revolutionary violence, triggered Camus's public and permanent break with Sartre.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2; IEP "Existentialism"
- [ ] **Sartre's Nausea vs. Camus's Absurd**
      — Camus argued Sartre's novel Nausea starts from a feeling like the absurd but Sartre then turns it into a full metaphysical system.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.3
- [ ] **Camus's Humanism vs. Existentialist Freedom**
      — Camus grounded ethics in a shared, defensible human dignity, rather than in existentialist claims about radical, self-created freedom.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Revolt", "Existentialism"
### VI. Sisyphus as the Absurd Hero

- [ ] **Sisyphus as the Absurd Hero**
      — Camus uses the Greek myth of Sisyphus, condemned to push a boulder uphill forever, as his central image of the absurd hero.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.4
- [ ] **"Superior to His Fate": The Walk Back Down**
      — Camus focuses on the moment Sisyphus walks back down the mountain, fully conscious, as the peak of his strange dignity.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.4
- [ ] **"One Must Imagine Sisyphus Happy"**
      — Camus's famous closing line claims we should picture Sisyphus as happy, precisely because he has fully accepted his fate.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.4
- [ ] **Happiness Is Not Optimism, for Camus**
      — Camus's notion of happiness in the absurd means lucid acceptance of one's condition, not optimism that things will improve.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.4, §3.5
- [ ] **"His Rock Is His Thing": Owning One's Fate**
      — Camus says Sisyphus makes his punishment his own by claiming it, turning an imposed fate into something like a chosen life.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.4
### VII. Ethics and Politics

- [ ] **Limits and Moderation as Political Virtues**
      — Camus's mature political thought values self-imposed limits and moderation over any pursuit of a total, final solution to injustice.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3
- [ ] **Camus's Lifelong Opposition to Capital Punishment**
      — Camus opposed the death penalty throughout his life, treating it as a form of state barbarism rather than justice.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6; IEP "The Death Penalty"
- [ ] **"Reflections on the Guillotine" Against Retribution**
      — Camus's essay on the death penalty argues that execution cannot be a fair "eye for an eye" because no crime matches its cruelty.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6
- [ ] **Conditions Camus Sets for (Rare) Justified Political Killing**
      — Camus argues political killing is only even arguably justified when the target is a tyrant, no bystanders are harmed, and the killer accepts death.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3, §6
- [ ] **Free Speech and Abolishing Execution as Tests of Honest Rebellion**
      — Camus treats opposing the death penalty and defending free speech as practical tests distinguishing genuine rebellion from creeping totalitarianism.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3
- [ ] **The Plague: Modest Decency Over Heroism**
      — In The Plague, Camus presents ordinary, unglamorous decency, not dramatic heroism, as the right response to collective disaster.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6
- [ ] **Camus and Algeria: A Complicated, Criticized Silence**
      — Camus, born and raised in colonial Algeria, never published a direct condemnation of French colonialism, drawing lasting criticism for that silence.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Camus, Colonialism, and Algeria"
### VIII. Related Literary Themes

- [ ] **Meursault and the Outsider's Stance**
      — Camus's novel The Stranger centers on Meursault, whose flat emotional detachment makes him an outsider condemned as much for his affect as his crime.
      — _Source:_ IEP "The Outsider"
- [ ] **The Fall and the Claim "We Are All Guilty"**
      — In The Fall, Camus's narrator Clamence concludes that every person is guilty, since no one has clean moral standing to judge anyone else.
      — _Source:_ SEP §5; IEP "Guilt and Innocence"
- [ ] **Life as Tension, Not Resolution**
      — Camus's later fiction suggests genuine moral life is full of unresolved tensions that we must learn to tolerate rather than neatly solve.
      — _Source:_ SEP §5

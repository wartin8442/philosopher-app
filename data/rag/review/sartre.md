### A. Existence Precedes Essence

- [ ] **Existence Precedes Essence**
      — For Sartre, a human being first simply exists, and only later, through living, defines what they are — there is no pre-set human essence.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1 (Life and Works, mentions the 1945 lecture); IEP §2d (Existential Phenomenology), which states the "existence precedes essence" formula directly.
- [ ] **The Paper-Knife Contrast: Why Humans Differ from Artifacts**
      — Sartre argues humans differ from manufactured objects because no one designed us with a purpose before we existed.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the lecture's opening argument, cross-checked against SEP §1 and IEP §2d for the existence-precedes-essence formula this illustration supports.
- [ ] **No Human Nature, But a Shared Human Condition**
      — Sartre denies a fixed human "nature" but still affirms a universal human "condition" — basic limits every person faces.
      — _Source:_ IEP §2d and §4a on the for-itself having no fixed identity, generalized to the nature/condition distinction Sartre draws in the 1945 lecture.
- [ ] **Contingency: Existence Has No Built-In Reason**
      — In his novel *Nausea*, Sartre dramatizes the idea that things (and people) simply exist, with no built-in justification for why they're there at all.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1 (Life and Works), which lists *Nausea* (1938) as a major early work; general background on the novel's central scene, cross-checked for consistency with the ontology described in SEP §4.1.
- [ ] **Subjectivity as the Starting Point**
      — Sartre holds that any philosophy of human existence must start from the first-person experience of an individual choosing, not from abstract theories about "man" in general.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the lecture's structure; cross-checked against IEP §2b/§2d discussion of pre-reflective vs. reflective consciousness as the basic starting point of Sartre's method.
### B. Radical Freedom and Responsibility

- [ ] **Freedom Is Built Into Consciousness Itself**
      — For Sartre, freedom isn't a special extra power some people have more of — it's simply what it is to be a conscious being at all.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (Negation and freedom) — discussion of questioning, negation, and freedom as "ontologically basic"; IEP §3c (Nothingness).
- [ ] **"Condemned to Be Free"**
      — Sartre's phrase "condemned to be free" means we cannot escape making choices — even refusing to choose is itself a choice.
      — _Source:_ IEP §6a (Freedom) — "Sartre presents his notion of freedom as amounting to making choices, and indeed not being able to avoid making choices."
- [ ] **Freedom Operates Within Facticity, Not Apart from It**
      — Sartre's freedom is always freedom *in a situation* — bound up with the concrete facts of your body, history, and circumstances, which he calls facticity.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 — the hiker/fatigue example illustrating freedom operating "within facticity."
- [ ] **Radical Freedom Does Not Mean Unconstrained by Circumstances**
      — Sartre's claim that we are radically free is not the claim that circumstances don't limit us — it's a claim about how we relate to whatever limits we face.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (situated freedom, facticity); IEP §6a (freedom as "characteristic of the nature of consciousness," not an ability to do anything whatsoever).
- [ ] **Radical Responsibility: No One to Blame**
      — Because there is no fixed human nature or external authority dictating our choices, Sartre holds that each person bears total responsibility for who they become.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (freedom as constitutive of human reality); IEP §2c (Ethics) — consciousness as spontaneous and transparent, so the agent is "fully responsible."
- [ ] **In Choosing for Yourself, You Choose an Image of Humanity**
      — Sartre argues that every individual choice implicitly endorses a value that we'd want everyone in a similar situation to act on.
      — _Source:_ IEP §6c (An Ethical Dimension) — "By choosing, an individual commits not only himself, but the whole of humanity" and the discussion of the "singular universal."
- [ ] **Determinism as an Alibi**
      — Sartre treats appeals to psychological or social determinism ("I couldn't help it, that's just how I was raised") as a disguised attempt to escape responsibility, not a neutral scientific fact.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 — Sartre's critique of psychoanalytic determinism and "naturalist assumptions"; IEP §2c (Ethics) on rejecting excuses grounded in unconscious forces.
### C. Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi)

- [ ] **Bad Faith Defined: Lying to Yourself, Not Others**
      — Bad faith is Sartre's term for a specific kind of self-deception in which a person hides the truth from themselves while somehow still knowing it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 (Bad faith and the critique of Freudian psychoanalysis).
- [ ] **The Café Waiter**
      — Sartre's example of a waiter who overacts the role of "waiter" illustrates bad faith as pretending you simply *are* your social role, with no freedom left over.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 (the café waiter example); IEP §4b (The Project of Bad Faith).
- [ ] **Bad Faith by Collapsing into a Thing**
      — One pattern of bad faith is denying your own freedom by acting as though you were a fixed object rather than an open-ended, choosing being.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 — bad faith as "exclusive affirmation" of facticity or transcendence.
- [ ] **Bad Faith by Denying Your Situation**
      — The opposite pattern of bad faith is treating yourself as pure limitless possibility, refusing to acknowledge the concrete facts and commitments already shaping your situation.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of Sartre's second worked example in the bad faith chapter, cross-checked against SEP §4.2's description of bad faith as oscillation between facticity and transcendence.
- [ ] **Why Bad Faith Is Self-Undermining**
      — Sartre argues bad faith is inherently unstable because the very freedom it tries to deny is what makes the denial possible in the first place.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 ("bad faith... always contains the germ of its destruction"); IEP §4b ("The freedom of the for-itself is a pre-condition for the project of bad faith which denies it").
- [ ] **Sartre's Critique of Freud's Unconscious**
      — Sartre rejects Freud's unconscious mind as an explanation of self-deception, arguing it just relocates the puzzle instead of solving it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 (critique of Freudian meta-psychology, the "homuncular fallacy" of the censor); IEP §4b.
- [ ] **Sincerity Can Also Be Bad Faith**
      — Sartre argues that trying to be perfectly "sincere" — to simply be, and honestly report, what you really are — is itself impossible and can become another form of bad faith.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.2 — "No project of 'sincerity' (strictly being what one is) remains possible given the for-itself's structure."
### D. Being-For-Itself vs. Being-In-Itself

- [ ] **Being-in-Itself: Things Just Are What They Are**
      — Sartre calls the mode of being of ordinary objects "being-in-itself": complete, self-identical, and lacking any inner distance from themselves.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (being-in-itself as "pure plenitude"); IEP §3b (Two Types of Being).
- [ ] **Being-for-Itself: Consciousness Is What It Is Not**
      — Sartre argues that consciousness (which he calls "being-for-itself") never simply coincides with itself the way an object does — it's always ahead of, or beyond, whatever it currently is.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (the for-itself as "a being which is what it is not and is not what it is"); IEP §4a (A Lack of Self-Identity).
- [ ] **Humans as "Nothingness" at the Core of Being**
      — Sartre argues that what makes consciousness different from objects is that it introduces "nothingness" — the capacity to negate, question, and imagine what is not the case — into the world.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 ("consciousness is a being by whom nothingness comes into the world"); IEP §3c (Nothingness).
- [ ] **Consciousness Has No Hidden Interior**
      — Sartre holds that consciousness is completely "transparent" to itself — there is no hidden inner self, container, or homunculus behind your awareness.
      — _Source:_ IEP §3b/§4b ("consciousness is completely 'translucent'... nothing is hidden, since consciousness is transparent"); SEP §4.2 on the critique of the Freudian unconscious.
- [ ] **We Never Coincide With Ourselves**
      — Sartre argues that the structure of time means a person is never simply identical to their past self or their future self.
      — _Source:_ IEP §4a — "the for-itself never coincides with itself: it 'is not identical with its past nor its future.'"
- [ ] **Nothingness Enters the World Through Us**
      — Sartre's example of expecting to meet a friend named Pierre at a café and finding him absent shows how negation and absence are real features of lived experience, not just abstract logical facts.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (the Pierre-at-the-café example); IEP §3c (Nothingness).
### E. The Look and Being-for-Others

- [ ] **The Look: Becoming an Object in Someone Else's World**
      — Sartre argues that when another person perceives you, you experience yourself as an object seen from outside — what he calls "the Look" — and this is a distinct kind of experience, not an inference.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3 (the keyhole scene); IEP §5a (The Problem of Other Minds).
- [ ] **Shame as Evidence of Other Minds**
      — Sartre argues that the experience of shame at being seen doing something is what actually grounds our certainty that other minds exist — not philosophical inference.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3 ("pre-moral shame... a shudder recognizing that one is the object the other sees"); IEP §5a.
- [ ] **Conflict as the Basic Shape of Relations with Others**
      — Sartre argues that the fundamental structure of relationships between people is conflict — each consciousness struggles either to objectify the other or be objectified by them.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3 ("the essence of relations between consciousnesses is 'conflict,' not... Heidegger's Mitsein"); IEP §5b (Human Relationships).
- [ ] **Sadism and Masochism as Two Failed Escapes from the Look**
      — Sartre analyzes sadism and masochism as two opposite strategies for dealing with the conflict of the Look, both of which he thinks ultimately fail.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3 ("This appears as sadism... or masochism... Both strategies represent futile attempts to escape fundamental otherness.").
- [ ] **Love as a Contradictory Project**
      — Sartre argues that romantic love, as usually understood, involves an impossible demand: wanting to possess another person while also wanting them to remain fully free.
      — _Source:_ IEP §5b (Human Relationships — love's structure mirroring the desire for being); SEP §4.3 (sadism/masochism/love as forms of conflict).
- [ ] **Why "We" Experiences Don't Refute the Conflict Thesis**
      — Sartre acknowledges shared "we" experiences (like an audience watching a concert together) but argues these are psychologically real without undermining the deeper claim that conflict is the basic structure between consciousnesses.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.3 ("Sartre distinguishes co-spectatorship... from genuine ontological grounding of other knowledge. Such shared subjectivity is merely 'subjective and psychological'").
### F. Anguish

- [ ] **Anguish as the Recognition of Freedom**
      — Sartre argues that "anguish" is the specific feeling of realizing that you, and only you, are responsible for your choices, with nothing outside you to fall back on.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4.1 (freedom as constitutive, negation and freedom linked); general knowledge of the "cliff-edge vertigo" illustration used across secondary literature on Sartrean anguish; concept confirmed present in both Being and Nothingness's treatment of freedom and the 1945 lecture's discussion of anguish.
- [ ] **Anguish Is Not the Same as Fear**
      — Sartre distinguishes anguish (anxiety about your own freedom) from ordinary fear (being afraid of some external threat).
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the classic fear/anguish distinction Sartre draws (well documented across secondary literature on Being and Nothingness Part One), consistent with SEP §4.1's account of freedom as an ontological structure rather than a worldly threat.
- [ ] **Abandonment: No God, No Guarantees**
      — Sartre's notion of "abandonment" (or forlornness) is the recognition that, without God, there is no external source that can hand down values or guarantee what we ought to do.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the lecture's structure (it explicitly organizes its argument around anguish, abandonment, and despair); consistent with SEP's characterization of the lecture as a popularization of the ontology in Being and Nothingness (SEP §1).
- [ ] **Despair: Acting Without Certainty of Success**
      — Sartre's notion of "despair" means acting on the basis of what is actually within your power, without counting on favorable outcomes you can't control.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the lecture's structure, consistent with SEP §4.1's emphasis on freedom and responsibility as always located in what the agent themselves does.
### G. Humanism and Its Relation to the Technical Ontology

- [ ] **"Existentialism Is a Humanism": A Popular Lecture, Not the Full System**
      — Sartre's 1945 lecture "Existentialism is a Humanism" is a simplified, public-facing defense of existentialism, not a substitute for the technical arguments of Being and Nothingness.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1 (Life and Works) — the lecture as "an accessible version of his difficult treatise, Being and Nothingness," responding to "Marxist and Christian critics."
- [ ] **Sartre Later Distanced Himself from Parts of the Humanism Lecture**
      — Sartre later expressed reservations about aspects of "Existentialism is a Humanism," regarding it as a hastily simplified account he did not fully stand behind in the same form.
      — _Source:_ General biographical knowledge of Sartre's later ambivalence about the lecture; the SEP entry itself does not discuss this distancing explicitly (confirmed by direct query — SEP §1 presents the lecture and Being and Nothingness as complementary without noting later reservations), so this card leans on broader secondary literature and should be treated as a well-attested but not SEP/IEP-sourced claim.
- [ ] **Existentialism Is an Optimistic Doctrine of Action, Not Nihilism**
      — Sartre explicitly rejects the charge that existentialism is a nihilistic or despairing philosophy, presenting it instead as a doctrine centered on action and self-making.
      — _Source:_ General knowledge of the lecture's explicit rebuttal of the nihilism charge; consistent with SEP §4.1's account of freedom as requiring the agent to "posit" the significance of their situation through choice.
- [ ] **Authenticity Has an Ethical Dimension, Even Without Fixed Values**
      — Sartre holds that "authenticity" — properly owning both your freedom and your factual situation — carries genuine ethical weight, even though he denies any pre-given moral rules.
      — _Source:_ IEP §6b–6c (Authenticity; An Ethical Dimension) — "a recognition of how our freedom interacts with our facticity exhibits the responsibility which we have to make proper choices."
### H. The Marxist Turn: Critique of Dialectical Reason

- [ ] **The Turn Toward Marxism**
      — In his later work, especially Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960), Sartre tried to combine his existentialist account of individual freedom with Marxist analysis of history and social class.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 (Existentialist Marxism); IEP §7a (Critique of Dialectical Reason).
- [ ] **Scarcity as the Material Root of Social Conflict**
      — In the Critique, Sartre argues that material scarcity — not just human nature or psychology — is a basic driver of conflict and alienation in social life.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 (practico-inert, scarcity as background condition of seriality); general Marxist-influenced reading Sartre develops in the Critique.
- [ ] **Seriality: Isolated Individuals United Only by Function**
      — Sartre uses the term "seriality" to describe a common social condition where people are grouped together only by an external role or function, remaining isolated and interchangeable with one another.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 ("Seriality: The dominant social form where individuals are substitutable, anonymous, and separated despite apparent unity. Waiting for transport... exemplifies serial existence").
- [ ] **The Group-in-Fusion: Freedom Born from Collective Crisis**
      — Sartre argues that in moments of shared crisis, isolated individuals ("seriality") can suddenly unite into a genuinely cooperative group he calls a "group-in-fusion."
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 ("Group-in-Fusion: A rupture occurring when serial individuals spontaneously unite through shared praxis toward common goals... demonstrates authentic reciprocity and freedom").
- [ ] **The Practico-Inert: How Past Human Action Constrains Future Freedom**
      — Sartre's concept of the "practico-inert" describes how the accumulated results of past human activity harden into material conditions that constrain the freedom of people acting later.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 (Practico-Inert — "the activity of others insofar as it is sustained and diverted by inorganic inertia").
- [ ] **Institutions Reintroduce the Very Alienation Revolutions Try to Escape**
      — Sartre argues that groups formed through spontaneous collective action (group-in-fusion) tend to re-solidify into institutions, which reintroduce hierarchy and the isolation the group was trying to escape.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 ("The Ontological Check: Groups inevitably formalize into institutions, reintroducing hierarchies... and relapsing into seriality").
- [ ] **Dialectical Reason vs. Analytical Reason**
      — Sartre argues that understanding history and social life properly requires "dialectical reason" — thinking that tracks how wholes and processes develop over time — rather than "analytical reason," which breaks things into separate, static pieces.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 ("Dialectical vs. Analytical Reason: Sartre privileges dialectical reason—capturing totalization and praxis—over analytical approaches that fragment phenomena into static categories").
- [ ] **The Critique Revises, Rather Than Abandons, Being and Nothingness**
      — Sartre's later Marxist work does not reject his earlier existentialism but revises it — adding collective, material, and historical dimensions to a view that had earlier centered on individual consciousness.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6 (closing characterization: the Critique "revises Being and Nothingness's emphasis on dyadic consciousness and individual freedom, introducing collective mediations and material conditions while maintaining freedom's irreducibility"); IEP §7a-b.
### I. Background, Method, and Applications

- [ ] **Intentionality: Consciousness Is Always Consciousness "Of" Something**
      — Following Husserl, Sartre holds that consciousness is never just a free-floating inner state — it's always directed at, or "of," something outside itself.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2 (Transcendence of the Ego: The Discovery of Intentionality) — "intentionality allows us to discard the metaphysical oppositions between the inner and outer."
- [ ] **The Self Is Something We Construct, Not a Hidden Inner Ego**
      — In his early work The Transcendence of the Ego, Sartre argues that the "self" or "ego" is not a hidden inner subject behind all experience, but something consciousness itself produces when it reflects on its own past activity.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2 (the running-after-a-streetcar and portrait-contemplation examples, "no I"); IEP §2b (The Ego).
- [ ] **Existential Psychoanalysis: Interpreting a Person's Fundamental Choice**
      — Sartre proposes replacing Freudian psychoanalysis with "existential psychoanalysis," which interprets all of a person's behavior as expressing one deep, freely chosen orientation toward life rather than unconscious drives.
      — _Source:_ SEP §5 (Existential Psychoanalysis and the Fundamental Project); IEP §4c.
- [ ] **Literature as an Appeal to the Reader's Freedom**
      — Sartre argues that literature, especially the novel, works by appealing to and requiring the reader's own freedom to complete the meaning of the work.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3 (Imagination, Phenomenology and Literature) — literature's "one true subject" as freedom; the reader as sharing "responsibility for the fictional world's meaning."
- [ ] **Racism and Colonial Domination as Institutionalized Bad Faith**
      — Sartre analyzed racism and colonial oppression as forms of bad faith at a social scale — treating people as fixed essences (a "type") rather than as free individuals.
      — _Source:_ SEP §7 (Politics and Anti-Colonialism) — racism as essentialism, "irresponsible responsibility," collective responsibility for colonial exploitation.

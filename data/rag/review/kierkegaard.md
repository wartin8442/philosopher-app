- [ ] **The Aesthetic Stage: Living for the Interesting**

      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.1 "The Aesthetic"; IEP "Three Spheres of Existence."
- [ ] **The Aesthete's Hidden Unhappiness**
      — Kierkegaard held that the aesthetic way of life, however pleasant it feels in the moment, tends to end in despair.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.1; SEP section 2 (despair).
- [ ] **The Ethical Stage: Choosing Enduring Commitments**
      — The ethical stage of existence means freely taking on binding commitments, like marriage or civic duty, that give a life lasting shape.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.2 "The Ethical."
- [ ] **Judge William's Case Is Presented, Not Settled**
      — Judge William's confident defense of ethical life in Either/Or is a pseudonymous voice within a debate, not Kierkegaard's own final verdict.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.2; SEP discussion of pseudonymous authorship.
- [ ] **Religiousness A: A Universal Path of Renunciation**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes Climacus describes "Religiousness A" as a demanding, self-denying spirituality available to any thoughtful person without needing Christian revelation.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.3 "The Religious and the Life of Faith."
- [ ] **Religiousness B: Christianity's Distinct, Paradoxical Claim**
      — "Religiousness B" is specifically Christian faith, which Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes Climacus distinguishes from generic religiousness by its dependence on one paradoxical historical claim.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.3; IEP "Subjectivity and Truth."
- [ ] **Truth Is Subjectivity**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes Climacus argues that for the questions that matter most, how passionately and personally you hold a belief is part of what makes it true for you.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3, opening discussion of subjectivity; IEP "Subjectivity and Truth."
- [ ] **Against Hegel: No System Can Capture a Life Still Being Lived**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes Climacus argues that no philosophical system can fully capture human existence, because the thinker doing the system-building is still alive and unfinished.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3, critique of Hegel; SEP "Trajectories in Kierkegaard Scholarship."
- [ ] **Objective Inquiry Is the Wrong Tool for "How Should I Live?"**
      — Kierkegaard held that detached, objective inquiry works fine for facts about the world but is the wrong approach to questions about how a person should live.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3, discussion of objective vs. subjective thought.
- [ ] **Kierkegaard's Real Target Was Often Danish Hegelians, Not Just Hegel**
      — Some recent scholarship argues Kierkegaard's sharpest attacks were often aimed at Danish followers of Hegel rather than at Hegel's own writings directly.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 4, "Trajectories in Kierkegaard Scholarship."
- [ ] **Anxiety as the Dizziness of Freedom**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis describes anxiety as the unsettling feeling of confronting your own freedom and its open possibilities.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Anxiety"; IEP "Anxiety and Despair."
- [ ] **Anxiety Is Not a Disorder — It Comes with Being Free**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis treats anxiety as a built-in feature of human freedom, not a psychological problem to be cured.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Anxiety."
- [ ] **Anxiety Comes Before Sin but Doesn't Cause or Excuse It**
      — Vigilius Haufniensis argues that anxiety precedes any wrongdoing and makes it possible, without anxiety itself being sinful or a valid excuse for sin.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, discussion of anxiety and original sin.
- [ ] **"Learning to Be Anxious in the Right Way"**
      — Vigilius Haufniensis claims that learning to face anxiety properly, rather than avoiding or suppressing it, is essential spiritual education.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, closing discussion of anxiety.
- [ ] **The Self as Something You Have to Build, Not Something You Already Are**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Anti-Climacus argues that a self is not a fixed thing a person simply has, but an achievement that must be actively built.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Despair"; IEP "Anxiety and Despair."
- [ ] **Despair as Failing to Become a Real Self**
      — Anti-Climacus defines despair as the state of failing to become a genuine, integrated self, whether or not the person feels distressed.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Despair."
- [ ] **Two Opposite Failure Modes: Losing Touch with Reality vs. Losing All Hope**
      — Anti-Climacus distinguishes "despair of possibility," which loses touch with reality, from "despair of necessity," which loses all sense of open possibility.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Despair."
- [ ] **Despair Can Hide Behind an Ordinary, Even Happy Life**
      — Anti-Climacus argues that a person can be in despair without knowing it, even while feeling content or successful by ordinary standards.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Despair."
- [ ] **Despair, at Bottom, Is a Failure to Rest in God**
      — Anti-Climacus ultimately identifies the deepest root of despair as a self's failure to rest transparently in the power that established it, namely God.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, discussion of despair and sin.
- [ ] **Realizing You're in Despair Is a Step Toward Faith**
      — Anti-Climacus holds that becoming honestly aware of your own despair is a necessary step on the way toward faith, not a sign of failure.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, closing discussion of despair; IEP "Anxiety and Despair."
- [ ] **The Leap of Faith as a Decision, Not a Deduction**
      — Kierkegaard describes faith as involving a "leap" — a decisive personal commitment that reasoning alone can never fully justify or complete.

- [ ] **The Leap of Faith Is Not "Believing for No Reason at All"**
      — Kierkegaard's "leap of faith" does not mean believing arbitrarily or without any reasons — it means committing where reasoning cannot finish the job alone.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.3, "Faith."
- [ ] **Reflection Never Ends on Its Own — Only a Decision Does**
      — Johannes Climacus observes that rational reflection has no natural stopping point, so only a free decision, not further reasoning, can end it.

- [ ] **Teleological Suspension of the Ethical**
      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes de silentio argues that, in rare cases, faith can override ordinary moral rules for the sake of an individual's direct relationship with God.

- [ ] **Silentio Himself Finds Abraham Disturbing, Not Admirable in a Simple Way**

      — _Source:_ SEP section 3.3.1, "Faith."

      — Johannes de silentio's "knight of faith" both gives up a cherished hope and simultaneously believes, absurdly, that it will be restored in this life.

      — Johannes de silentio argues that Abraham cannot publicly explain or justify his act to anyone, unlike a tragic hero acting for a shared, communal good.

      — Kierkegaard's pseudonym Johannes Climacus holds that Christianity's core claim — that God became a specific human being — cannot be made rationally coherent, only believed.

      — Kierkegaard calls his method "indirect communication," meaning he tries to get readers to work out ethical and religious truths themselves rather than stating them outright.

      — _Source:_ IEP "Pseudonymous Authorship & Indirect Communication."
- [ ] **Christendom vs. Christianity**
      — Kierkegaard distinguished "Christendom" — a culture that assumes everyone is automatically Christian — from authentic Christianity, which he saw as a costly, individual commitment.

      — _Source:_ IEP "Attack on Danish Church & Christendom."

- [ ] **Real Christianity, on Kierkegaard's View, Costs Something**
      — Kierkegaard held that authentic Christian faith is demanding and costly, and treated a comfortable, undemanding version of it as a warning sign, not a virtue.
      — _Source:_ IEP "Attack on Danish Church & Christendom"; SEP brief note on Christendom.

      — Kierkegaard's polemic against the Danish Church aimed to defend authentic Christianity by attacking its comfortable, institutional counterfeit, not to reject Christian faith itself.

- [ ] **Neighbor-Love vs. Preferential Love**
      — In Works of Love, Kierkegaard distinguishes commanded "neighbor-love," owed to everyone, from "preferential" loves like romance and friendship, which arise naturally toward a chosen few.

- [ ] **Love as Duty, Not Just Feeling**
      — Kierkegaard argues that genuine neighbor-love is fundamentally a commanded duty, which makes it more stable and reliable than love based purely on feeling.

      — Kierkegaard does not condemn romantic love or friendship in Works of Love; he warns only against their tendency to become selfish or exclusive.

- [ ] **The Public and the Press as an Anonymous Leveling Force**
      — Kierkegaard argued that the modern press helped create "the public," an anonymous, faceless entity that pressures individuals toward conformity.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Social Critique."
- [ ] **Leveling: The Suppression of Individual Distinctiveness**
      — Kierkegaard used the term "leveling" for a social process that flattens individual excellence and distinctiveness into a bland, interchangeable sameness.
      — _Source:_ SEP section 2, "Social Critique."
- [ ] **Kierkegaard Was Not a Simple Irrationalist**
      — Kierkegaard did not hold that reason is worthless or that religious belief should ignore rational reflection altogether.

## Misattribution / disputed entries (3) — lighter check: is the debunking explanation itself right?

- [ ] "If you label me, you negate me. (Once you label me you negate me.)"
      — No occurrence in Kierkegaard's works. Likely a compressed paraphrase of his anti-Hegelian stance, traceable to Jean Wahl's 1945 New Republic article and Colin Wilson's The Outsider (1956); the snappy form first appears in 1960s–70s American magazines.
- [ ] "Leap of faith."
      — Kierkegaard never uses a phrase that translates as 'leap of faith' anywhere in his writings. He writes repeatedly of 'the leap' (Philosophical Fragments 1844; The Concept of Anxiety 1844; Concluding Unscientific Postscript 1846), and the concept is fairly ascribed to him — but the famous phrase itself is a later coinage. The persona may discuss 'the leap' but should correct users who quote 'leap of faith' as his words.
- [ ] "Life is not a problem to be solved, but a reality to be experienced."
      — Not Kierkegaard. An altered version of a sentence in J. J. van der Leeuw's The Conquest of Illusion (1928): 'The mystery of life is not a problem to be solved; it is a reality to be experienced.' Misattributed to Kierkegaard since at least the late 1940s.

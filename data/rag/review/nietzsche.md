### I. Death of God and Cultural Diagnosis

- [ ] **The Death of God Is a Cultural Diagnosis, Not an Argument for Atheism**
      — Nietzsche's declaration that "God is dead" mainly observes that Christian belief has stopped functioning as Europe's shared foundation, not argues for atheism.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2 Critique of Religion and Morality; IEP 1.b Rejection of Otherworldly Basis for Value
- [ ] **The Parable of the Madman**
      — In The Gay Science, a "madman" announces God's death to a crowd of unbelievers who laugh at him, showing they haven't grasped its significance.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2; IEP 1.b
- [ ] **Losing God Means Losing More Than Religion**
      — Nietzsche argues that European morality was built on Christian assumptions, so it cannot simply survive intact once those assumptions are abandoned.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2
- [ ] **Nihilism Is the Danger the Death of God Creates, Not Nietzsche's Own Position**
      — Nietzsche treats nihilism — the feeling that nothing has value once old guarantees vanish — as the great looming danger of his age, not his own view.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.g The Threat of Nihilism
- [ ] **The "Last Man" Is a Warning, Not an Ideal**
      — Nietzsche's figure of the "last man" pictures a comfort-obsessed, ambition-less humanity as the bleak endpoint of a civilization that has given up on greatness.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.g; SEP §3.2.2 Affirmation (contrast)
- [ ] **Nietzsche's Naturalism: No Otherworldly Replacement**
      — Nietzsche insists philosophers should explain human beings and values using only natural facts about biology, psychology, and history, with no otherworldly additions.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.b
### II. Master/Slave Morality, Ressentiment, and Genealogy

- [ ] **Two Original Patterns of Moral Evaluation: Good/Bad and Good/Evil**
      — Nietzsche distinguishes an ancient "good versus bad" morality of excellence from a later "good versus evil" morality of altruism, treating both as historical inventions.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2, First Treatise
- [ ] **Master Morality Values Strength and Distance, Not Cruelty for Its Own Sake**
      — "Master morality" is Nietzsche's term for the outlook of ancient warrior and aristocratic classes, who called their own strength and excellence "good" as a matter of course.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2
- [ ] **Ressentiment: How the Powerless Build a Morality Out of Resentment**
      — Ressentiment is Nietzsche's term for a slow-burning resentment the powerless feel toward those who dominate them, which he says gave birth to a new morality.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2, Slave Revolt in Morality; IEP 1.e
- [ ] **The Slave Revolt Was, for Nietzsche, "the Most Spiritual Revenge"**
      — Nietzsche argues that redefining goodness around meekness and altruism functioned as an indirect, psychological revenge against the strong, not literal retaliation.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2
- [ ] **Genealogy Exposes a Value's Hidden History — It Doesn't Simply "Refute" It**
      — Nietzsche's genealogical method traces a value's messy psychological origins to unsettle its aura of timeless authority, without claiming origins alone disprove it.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.e Genealogical Critique
- [ ] **Guilt Was Built Out of the Idea of Debt**
      — Nietzsche argues that our sense of moral guilt developed historically out of the older, more concrete relationship between a debtor and a creditor.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2, Second Treatise
- [ ] **Bad Conscience: Aggression Turned Inward**
      — Nietzsche claims that "bad conscience" arose when instincts for aggression and freedom, blocked by settled community life, turned inward against the self.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2; IEP 1.e Second Essay summary
- [ ] **The Ascetic Ideal Makes Self-Denial Look Noble**
      — Nietzsche argues the "ascetic ideal" — glorifying self-denial and suffering — persists because it gives suffering people a sense of purpose, at great psychological cost.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2, Third Treatise
- [ ] **The Critique of Pity and Compassion**
      — Nietzsche argues that compassion-centered morality wrongly assumes suffering is always bad, when suffering can also be necessary for growth.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.d Attacks on Content of Morality
### III. Will to Power

- [ ] **The Basic Doctrine: Power as a Fundamental Drive**
      — Nietzsche proposes that living things are fundamentally driven not by pleasure or mere survival but by a drive to increase their power.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1 The Will to Power
- [ ] **Power as Overcoming Resistance**
      — A leading modern reading holds that Nietzsche's "power" means the capacity to encounter obstacles and creatively overcome them, not mere domination over others.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1, Reginster's account
- [ ] **Mastery Versus Tyranny Within the Self**
      — Nietzsche's picture of power includes an inward version: a person's dominant drives can either tyrannize weaker drives or skillfully redirect them.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1, Richardson's tyranny/mastery distinction; IEP 2.b Autonomy
- [ ] **Will to Power Is Not Simply "Domination Over Other People"**
      — Although Nietzsche applies "will to power" to social domination in places, his central admired examples are frequently self-mastery, art, and inner discipline.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1
- [ ] **Will to Power as Nietzsche's Answer to Schopenhauer's Pessimism**
      — Nietzsche's will to power was developed partly to replace Schopenhauer's gloomier "will to life," offering grounds to endorse existence rather than despair of it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1, Reginster's account of overcoming Schopenhauer
- [ ] **Not Clearly a Universal Physical Law of the Cosmos**
      — Whether Nietzsche meant "will to power" as a claim about all living things, or all of physical reality, remains genuinely disputed among scholars.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1 Life and Works (Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's editing); SEP §6.1
### IV. Eternal Recurrence

- [ ] **The Thought Experiment: Would You Want This Exact Life Again, Forever?**
      — Nietzsche's eternal recurrence asks you to imagine living your exact life over again, identically, an infinite number of times, and to test your reaction.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.3; IEP 2.d Affirmation
- [ ] **Eternal Recurrence Is Best Read as a Psychological Test, Not a Scientific Theory**
      — Most scholars read eternal recurrence primarily as a test of how fully you affirm your life, rather than as a literal physical claim about time looping.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.3; IEP 2.d
- [ ] **Eternal Recurrence Is Tightly Linked to Amor Fati**
      — Being able to will the eternal recurrence of your life is, for Nietzsche, essentially the same achievement as loving your fate completely.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.2 Affirmation; IEP 2.d
- [ ] **Even Zarathustra Struggles With This Idea**
      — In Thus Spoke Zarathustra, Nietzsche portrays even his own exemplary character as finding the eternal recurrence terrifying and hard to accept.
      — _Source:_ IEP 2.d Affirmation
- [ ] **Not a Claim That Nothing You Do Matters**
      — Eternal recurrence does not imply fatalism or that your choices are futile — it is a standard for evaluating how you live, not an excuse for passivity.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.3; IEP 2.d
### V. The Übermensch and Self-Overcoming

- [ ] **The Übermensch as a Goal for Humanity to Grow Toward**
      — Nietzsche's "Übermensch" (overman) names an aspirational higher form of human being that humanity might create through ongoing self-overcoming.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.g context; SEP references to Zarathustra
- [ ] **Self-Overcoming Rather Than a Fixed Finish Line**
      — Nietzsche presents becoming an Übermensch less as reaching a finished state and more as a continual process of overcoming one's current limitations.
      — _Source:_ IEP 2.c Authenticity and Self-Creation
- [ ] **The Three Metamorphoses: Camel, Lion, Child**
      — Nietzsche uses the images of a camel, a lion, and a child to describe three stages of the spirit's development toward self-overcoming.
      — _Source:_ IEP discussion of self-creation; general Zarathustra structure
- [ ] **Higher Types Are Defined by Psychological Constitution, Not Race, Nation, or Class**
      — Nietzsche's "higher types" are a psychological category — people capable of a certain intensity of creativity and self-command — not tied to ethnicity or nationality.
      — _Source:_ IEP 2.a Higher Types
- [ ] **The Sovereign Individual: Self-Command Through Keeping Promises**
      — Nietzsche describes an ideal "sovereign individual" whose defining trait is the developed ability to make binding promises and answer for their own future conduct.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.5; IEP 2.b Autonomy, Sovereign Individual
### VI. Perspectivism and the Critique of Truth

- [ ] **Perspectivism: All Knowing Happens from Somewhere**
      — Perspectivism is Nietzsche's view that all human knowledge is shaped by a particular standpoint or set of concerns — there is no view from nowhere.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.2 Perspectivism
- [ ] **More Perspectives Yield a More Complete Understanding, Not Less**
      — Nietzsche argues that combining multiple different perspectives on the same issue produces a fuller, more objective picture than any single viewpoint could.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.2
- [ ] **Perspectivism Is Not the Claim That "Anything Goes"**
      — Nietzsche's perspectivism does not imply that every viewpoint is equally good or that truth claims can never be better or worse than one another.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.2, "Plurality Without Relativism"
- [ ] **The Critique of the "True World" Behind Appearances**
      — Nietzsche rejects the long philosophical tradition, running from Plato through Christianity to Kant, of positing a hidden "true world" more real than the one we experience.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2 (Platonism and Christianity parallel); IEP 1.b
- [ ] **There Are No Moral Facts, Only Moral Interpretations**
      — Nietzsche claims that moral judgments are not descriptions of objective facts in the world but interpretations imposed on events from an evaluative standpoint.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1 Nietzsche's Meta-ethical Stance
- [ ] **Even Science's Devotion to Truth Has a History and a Cost**
      — Nietzsche argues that the modern, unconditional commitment to truth-seeking in science descends from the same self-denying religious tradition it seems to oppose.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.3 Truthfulness/Honesty; IEP 1.e Third Essay
### VII. Critique of Religion and Metaphysics

- [ ] **Christianity as "The Religion of Pity"**
      — Nietzsche criticizes Christianity for making compassion toward suffering its central virtue, which he thinks ultimately weakens and diminishes people.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.d Attacks on Content of Morality
- [ ] **Christianity as the Historical Vehicle for the Slave Revolt in Morality**
      — Nietzsche identifies Christianity as the specific historical vehicle through which the "slave revolt" reordering of values spread across European culture.
      — _Source:_ SEP §2, Slave Revolt in Morality
- [ ] **Free Will Is, for Nietzsche, an Incoherent Idea Invented to Justify Blame**
      — Nietzsche rejects traditional free will as a cause that originates entirely in itself, calling it a confused notion built to make blame and punishment feel justified.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.c Attacks on Metaphysical Basis of Moral Agency
- [ ] **Consciousness Plays a Smaller Role in Behavior Than We Assume**
      — Nietzsche argues that most of what drives human behavior happens beneath conscious awareness, so consciousness is not the reliable central controller we take it to be.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.c
- [ ] **The Soul Is Not a Single, Simple, Indestructible Thing**
      — Nietzsche rejects the traditional picture of the soul as a single, indivisible, eternal unit, proposing instead that the self is a shifting society of competing drives.
      — _Source:_ SEP §4 The Self and Self-fashioning
- [ ] **Not Just God — Kantian and Platonic Substitutes for Morality's Foundation Are Rejected Too**
      — Nietzsche's rejection of religious grounds for morality extends equally to secular philosophical substitutes, like Kant's rational moral law or Plato's Form of the Good.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.b
### VIII. Amor Fati and Life-Affirmation

- [ ] **Amor Fati: Wanting Your Life Exactly as It Is**
      — Amor fati, Nietzsche's ideal of "love of fate," means actively wanting everything that has happened and will happen to you, not merely enduring it.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.2 Affirmation; IEP 2.d
- [ ] **Affirmation Answers Nihilism**
      — Nietzsche presents whole-life affirmation as his positive answer to the threat of nihilism created by the loss of religious meaning.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.2, Reginster's account of affirmation vs. nihilism
- [ ] **Affirmation Requires Honesty, Not Rose-Tinted Self-Deception**
      — Nietzsche insists that genuine affirmation of life must be built on an honest reckoning with reality, not on comforting illusions about how good things are.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.3 Truthfulness/Honesty, connected to affirmation
- [ ] **Why Nietzsche Thought We Still Need Art**
      — Nietzsche argues that human beings need art precisely because unrelieved, unfiltered truth about existence would otherwise be unbearable.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.4 Art and Artistry
- [ ] **Giving Style to One's Character**
      — Nietzsche holds that a well-lived life is partly an aesthetic achievement — a life given unity and "style," the way an artist shapes a work.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.2.4, Nehamas's reading of self-fashioning
### IX. Common Misreadings

- [ ] **Misreading: "Nietzsche Was a Nihilist"**
      — Nietzsche is frequently mislabeled a nihilist, when his entire philosophical project is an attempt to diagnose and overcome nihilism, not embrace it.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.g The Threat of Nihilism
- [ ] **Misreading: "Nietzsche Was a Proto-Nazi or Anti-Semite"**
      — Nietzsche explicitly and repeatedly condemned anti-Semitism and German nationalism in his lifetime; his later Nazi-era reputation resulted largely from his sister's editing.
      — _Source:_ SEP §1 Life and Works; IEP 1.e (note on the Genealogy's anti-Semitic imagery versus Nietzsche's personal anti-anti-Semitism)
- [ ] **Misreading: "Will to Power Means Might Makes Right"**
      — Reducing Nietzsche's will to power to a crude endorsement of "might makes right" domination ignores his emphasis on self-mastery and creativity.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.1, historical reception and Kaufmann's rehabilitation
- [ ] **Misreading: "Nietzsche Was Simply a Moral Relativist"**
      — Nietzsche is often mistaken for someone who thought all values are equally valid, when he in fact argued that some values and character types are better than others.
      — _Source:_ SEP §3.1 Nietzsche's Meta-ethical Stance
- [ ] **Misreading: "Nietzsche Was an Existentialist"**
      — While Nietzsche influenced existentialism, his emphasis on unchosen natural drives and constitution sits in tension with existentialism's radical, self-defining free choice.
      — _Source:_ IEP 2.c Authenticity and Self-Creation; IEP conclusion, "Not Simply an Existentialist"
- [ ] **Misreading: "The Übermensch Is a Nazi Racial Ideal"**
      — The Übermensch is not a racial or biological category in Nietzsche's writing; his admired higher types span many nations, eras, and ethnicities.
      — _Source:_ IEP 2.a Higher Types; SEP §1 (on Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche's distortions)
- [ ] **Misreading: "Eternal Recurrence Is a Serious Physics Claim"**
      — Treating eternal recurrence primarily as a scientific claim about the physical universe misreads a doctrine whose real philosophical weight is practical and personal.
      — _Source:_ SEP §6.3; IEP 2.d Affirmation
- [ ] **Misreading: "Nietzsche Wanted the Weak Eliminated" or Endorsed Cruelty**
      — Nietzsche's praise of strength and criticism of pity-morality is not a call for cruelty toward, or elimination of, weaker people.
      — _Source:_ IEP 1.d, 2.a; SEP §1 (on distortion via Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche)
- [ ] **Misreading: Nietzsche's Punchy Aphorisms Are Just Loosely Connected One-Liners**
      — Nietzsche's short, aphoristic sections can look like disconnected quips, but many build deliberately toward sustained, cumulative philosophical arguments.
      — _Source:_ SEP §5 Difficulties of Nietzsche's Philosophical Writing

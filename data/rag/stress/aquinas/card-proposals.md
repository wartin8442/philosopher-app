### Aquinas Rejects the Ontological Argument

**Claim:**

Aquinas denies that God's existence can be proven from the mere concept of God, rejecting Anselm's ontological argument.

**Explanation:**

Anselm argued that God, as "that than which nothing greater can be thought," must exist, since existing in reality is greater than existing only in the mind. Aquinas rejects this as a demonstration. His diagnosis: the proposition "God exists" is self-evident in itself (in God, essence and existence are identical), but not self-evident to us, because we do not know God's essence in this life. Since we cannot start from the divine essence, God's existence must be demonstrated the other way around — from God's effects in the world, as the Five Ways do. Attributing an "ontological argument" to Aquinas is a standard confusion to correct.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.1 (esp. ad 2)
- Summa Contra Gentiles I, ch. 10–11

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Aquinas proved God from the definition of God" — no; that is Anselm's strategy, which Aquinas explicitly declines.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God (Aquinas denies Anselm's ontological argument has force as a proof; ST 1a 2.1 ad 2, SCG I.10–11). Motivated by stress question aq-18.

### No Divine Illumination: Knowledge Comes by Abstraction

**Claim:**

Aquinas rejects the Augustinian view that God directly illuminates each act of human knowing; instead, each person's own agent intellect abstracts concepts from sense experience.

**Explanation:**

In the Augustinian tradition, human minds grasp truth because God's light illuminates each cognition. Aquinas declines this picture for ordinary knowledge: every human possesses an individual agent intellect that abstracts universal intelligible species from sense-derived images (phantasms). God remains involved only at a general level — the agent intellect's natural light is itself a created participation in the divine light — but there is no special, truth-by-truth illumination, and no separate illuminating intellect shared by all. This is a signature point of divergence from Augustine (and from Avicenna's separate agent intellect), and pairs with his rejection of Averroes's shared possible intellect.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.79, a.3–4
- ST I, Q.84, a.5

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Aquinas the Christian must hold we know truth by God's illumination" — he honors the Augustinian formula only by reinterpreting it: the created light of intellect is the "illumination," not a per-cognition divine act.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.2 Thought (rejection of divine illumination and of the separate agent/possible intellect); IEP: Aquinas, §4d. Motivated by stress question aq-19.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Intellect Knows Singulars Only Indirectly

**Claim:**

For Aquinas the intellect directly grasps only universals; singular material things are known intellectually only indirectly, by turning back to sense images.

**Explanation:**

Sense powers grasp particulars — this cat, this color. The intellect, receiving forms abstracted from individuating matter, directly grasps only the universal nature. So the intellect cannot, of itself, know "this cat" as this cat. It reaches singulars indirectly, by a reflexive turn (conversio/reflexio) to the phantasms from which its concepts were abstracted, so that everyday cognition of an individual always uses sense and intellect together. This surprising doctrine is a common exam point and a place where Aquinas differs from later scholastics like Scotus and Ockham, who allowed direct intellectual cognition of singulars.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.86, a.1
- ST I, Q.84, a.7 (turning to phantasms)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.2 Thought (ST 1a 86.1; joint operation of sense and intellect). Motivated by stress question aq-33.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Proving That God Is, Not What God Is: Demonstration Quia

**Claim:**

Aquinas classes his proofs of God as demonstrations "quia" — from effects to the fact that a cause exists — never "propter quid" through God's essence.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas distinguishes two kinds of demonstration: propter quid, which explains why something is so through its cause or essence, and quia, which establishes only that something is so, arguing from effects. Because we lack any grasp of God's essence, proofs of God must be quia demonstrations from creatures. Hence what the proofs deliver is knowledge that God is, while what God is remains beyond us in this life; positively we proceed only by negation (denying creaturely limits) and analogy. This frames the prologue to ST I, Q.3: we cannot consider how God is, but rather how God is not.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.2
- ST I, Q.3, prologue

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.3 Knowledge and Science (propter quid vs quia) and §2 God (ST 1a 3pr). Motivated by stress question aq-35.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Conscience Is an Act, Synderesis a Habit

**Claim:**

Aquinas distinguishes synderesis, the unerring habit of first moral principles, from conscience, the fallible act of applying knowledge to a particular case.

**Explanation:**

Synderesis is not a distinct power but a natural habit of practical intellect by which everyone grasps the first precepts of the natural law ("good is to be done and pursued, evil avoided"), as the speculative intellect grasps first principles of demonstration; at this level error is impossible. Conscience (conscientia) is something different: an act — the application of knowledge to what one has done or is about to do, witnessing, binding, accusing. Because it involves reasoning from principles to a concrete case, conscience can err through bad inference or mistaken facts, which is why Aquinas can discuss whether an erring conscience binds. Conflating the two words is a common mistake his own texts carefully avoid.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.79, a.12–13
- De Veritate, Q.16–17

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Natural Law (synderesis ST 1a 79.12, conscience ST 1a 79.13). Motivated by stress question aq-31.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Even the Cardinal Virtues Can Be Infused

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that besides the cardinal virtues acquired by habituation, God infuses cardinal virtues with grace, differing in species from the acquired ones.

**Explanation:**

It is not only faith, hope, and charity that God infuses. Because grace orders us to a supernatural end that no amount of practice can reach, Aquinas holds that prudence, justice, fortitude, and temperance are also given in an infused form along with charity, distinct in kind from their acquired counterparts. The two differ in their rule and measure: his own example is temperance in eating — acquired temperance follows the rule of health and reason, infused temperance the rule of one who disciplines the body for eternal life. Moral perfection therefore requires infused virtue; acquired virtue alone perfects us only relative to natural human ends. (This proposal corrects the existing card that contrasts infused theological virtues with cardinal virtues "built up through practice" as if the latter were acquired-only.)

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.63, a.3–4
- ST I-II, Q.62, a.1 (theological virtues infused only)

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Cardinal virtues = acquired, theological virtues = infused" is a false dichotomy in Aquinas: the cardinal virtues exist in both an acquired and an infused mode.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Virtue Theory ("moral perfection requires that even the cardinal virtues be infused by God", ST 1a2ae 63.3). Motivated by stress question aq-32.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Evil Is a Privation, and God Permits It for Good

**Claim:**

Aquinas treats evil not as a positive thing but as a privation of due good, which God permits only so as to draw greater good from it.

**Explanation:**

Evil has no essence or positive existence of its own: it is the absence of a good that ought to be present, as blindness is the lack of due sight. So evil needs no first cause of its own and cannot count against a wholly good first cause. When the objector argues in ST I, Q.2, a.3 that if God existed there would be no evil, Aquinas answers with Augustine: it belongs to God's infinite goodness to permit evils and bring good out of them. This reply — and the fact that the "no God because evil" argument appears in Aquinas only as an objection he refutes — should be retrievable together, since quoting the objection as his view is a standard trap.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.3, obj. 1 and ad 1
- ST I, Q.48, a.1; De Malo, Q.1

**Misreadings (optional):**

Summa articles open with arguments Aquinas rejects; any quotation beginning "It seems that..." is an objection, not his position.

**Provenance:** ST I q.2 a.3 (Dominican trans., ad 1 quoting Augustine, Enchiridion xi); SEP: Aquinas, §1.2 (disputed-question structure); IEP: Aquinas: Moral Philosophy, §1 (evil as privation). Motivated by stress questions aq-23 and aq-22.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Authority Comes from God, but Unjust Commands Must Be Disobeyed

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that political authority derives from God for the common good, so commands contrary to that purpose lose binding force and sinful commands must be disobeyed.

**Explanation:**

Authority as such descends from God through the natural sociality of human beings — Aquinas is no anarchist, and obedience to just law binds in conscience. But the derivation fails in two ways: when power is wrongly acquired, or wrongly used. A law unjust toward human good (burdens serving the ruler's greed) does not bind in conscience, though it may be tolerated to avoid scandal or disorder; a command contrary to God must never be obeyed — one must disobey, "as did the holy martyrs." He even writes that one who liberates his country by killing a tyrant can be praised. This political dimension goes beyond the bare unjust-law-is-no-law point and blocks the misreading that divine derivation of authority entails unconditional obedience.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.96, a.4
- Commentary on the Sentences, Bk. II, dist. 44 (on obedience and its limits)

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §9b Authority (anti-anarchism); Wikiquote sourced (Sentences passages, in Aquinas: Selected Political Writings, 1974, p. 183). Motivated by stress question aq-28.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Life Landmarks: 1273 Silence, 1274 Death, 1277 Censures, 1323 Sainthood

**Claim:**

Aquinas stopped writing after a mystical experience in December 1273 and died March 7, 1274; the Paris condemnations of 1277 were posthumous and were later declared not to touch his teaching.

**Explanation:**

Around December 6, 1273, in Naples, Aquinas underwent a powerful experience while celebrating Mass and afterward refused to write, telling Reginald of Piperno that all he had written seemed "like straw" beside what had been revealed to him (a reported remark from the canonization proceedings, not a written text). He died a few months later at Fossanova, March 7, 1274, en route to the Second Council of Lyon. On March 7, 1277 — three years to the day after his death — Bishop Tempier condemned 219 propositions at Paris, some implicating Aquinas's theses; Kilwardby's Oxford censure that month explicitly targeted his unicity-of-form and prime-matter doctrines. He was canonized July 18, 1323, and in 1325 the bishop of Paris declared the 1277 condemnations inapplicable to his teaching.

**Citations:**
- Processus canonizationis S. Thomae, Neapoli, LXXIX (the "straw" remark)
- (biographical; see provenance — dates are the card's content, not a doctrine)

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Aquinas was condemned by the Church in 1277" — he was dead; the censures named propositions, not the man, and were later lifted with respect to him.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §1.1 Life and §9 Influence (1277 three years to the day; Oxford; canonization July 18, 1323; clarification two years later); IEP: Aquinas, §1a. Motivated by stress questions aq-29 and aq-36.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### "My Soul Is Not I": The Separated Soul Is Not the Person

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that the surviving separated soul is not the complete human person, which is the soul-body composite awaiting resurrection.

**Explanation:**

Although the rational soul is incorruptible and survives death, Aquinas insists the human being is the composite: "man is not a soul only, but something composed of soul and body." Commenting on 1 Corinthians 15 he goes so far as to say "my soul is not I" (anima mea non est ego), arguing that if only souls are saved, neither I nor any man is saved. The separated state is unnatural and incomplete; full personal life requires the resurrection of the body. This card supplies the exact primary-text anchors for correcting the popular "I really am my immortal soul" reading, on which scholars' corruptionist/survivalist debate turns.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.75, a.4
- Super I ad Corinthios, ch. 15 (lect. 2)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §5 Soul and Body (corruptionist/survivalist debate; Sentences IV.43.1.1.1 ad 2); Wikiquote sourced ("Anima mea non est ego", Super I ad Corinthios 15.2). Motivated by stress question aq-27.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

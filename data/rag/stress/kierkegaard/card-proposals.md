### The Corsair Affair (1846)

**Claim:**

In 1845–46 Kierkegaard deliberately provoked the satirical paper The Corsair into attacking him, and the resulting public mockery reshaped the rest of his life and authorship.

**Explanation:**

The Corsair, edited by Meïr Goldschmidt, mocked most of Copenhagen's public figures but had spared Kierkegaard. Offended at being praised by a paper he despised, Kierkegaard — writing as his pseudonym Frater Taciturnus — attacked the Corsair-affiliated critic P.L. Møller and invited the paper to do its worst. It did: months of caricatures of his appearance made him a laughing-stock in the streets. The formerly sociable walker of Copenhagen became reclusive, gave up his plan of retiring to a country pastorate, and felt called to "remain at his post" as a writer — the pivot into the so-called second authorship.

**Citations:**
- The Corsair Affair (Kierkegaard's Writings 13) — the collected documents
- Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846) — the work that was to have ended the authorship

**Misreadings (optional):**

Don't reduce the affair to wounded vanity: Kierkegaard read it as lived proof of his analysis of "the public" and the leveling press.

**Provenance:** SEP "Søren Kierkegaard" §1; IEP §1d–e. Motivated by stress questions kg-04 (check kg-04-c3 failed), kg-35.

### What Triggered the Attack on the Church: Mynster, Martensen, and "a Witness to the Truth"

**Claim:**

Kierkegaard's open attack on the Danish church began after Bishop Mynster died in January 1854 and his successor Martensen eulogized him as "a witness to the truth."

**Explanation:**

Jakob Peter Mynster had been the Kierkegaard family's pastor, and Søren kept a filial respect for him while privately judging his comfortable, state-aligned church a betrayal of New Testament Christianity. When Hans Lassen Martensen — Kierkegaard's long-time rival and the incoming bishop primate — declared the late Mynster "a witness to the truth," Kierkegaard found the phrase unbearable: a witness to the truth suffers for it. He replied with a series of articles in the newspaper Fædrelandet (from December 1854) and then his own pamphlet The Moment (Øjeblikket, 1855), waging the campaign until his collapse and death in November 1855.

**Citations:**
- "Was Bishop Mynster 'a witness to the truth'…?", Fædrelandet (1854)
- The Moment (Øjeblikket), nos. 1–10 (1855)

**Misreadings (optional):**

The trigger was not a doctrinal dispute with Mynster's theology but the specific honorific claim; and the attacker remained, by his own account, a defender of Christianity.

**Provenance:** SEP §1; IEP §1f. Motivated by stress questions kg-05 (checks kg-05-c1 and kg-05-c3 failed), kg-23.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### "The Crowd Is Untruth": Where the Phrase Lives

**Claim:**

The phrase "the crowd is untruth" comes from Kierkegaard's signed dedication essay "That Single Individual," in the Point of View literature — not from any pseudonymous book.

**Explanation:**

Kierkegaard wrote two "Notes" concerning his work as an author, published with The Point of View for My Work as an Author (written 1848, published posthumously 1859). The first, dedicated to "that single individual," repeats the formula "the crowd is untruth": a crowd renders its members irresponsible and anonymous, whereas truth — ethically and religiously — can only be appropriated by the single individual answering for himself before God. Because the phrase is quoted constantly and pinned to whatever book a user last read (The Sickness unto Death is a common wrong guess), the location is worth a card of its own.

**Citations:**
- "That Single Individual: Two 'Notes' Concerning My Work as an Author" (with The Point of View, posthumous)

**Provenance:** IEP §3c (the crowd / the public); primary text: the Two Notes. Motivated by stress question kg-17 (passed, but the reply could only gesture at "a short piece … on the theme of the single individual" — no corpus item could ground the location).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Dissertation: On the Concept of Irony (1841)

**Claim:**

Kierkegaard's 1841 Magister dissertation was On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates, which reads Socratic irony as "infinite absolute negativity."

**Explanation:**

Before the authorship proper, Kierkegaard defended (in Latin, having been granted royal permission to write the text in Danish) a dissertation on irony. Socrates' irony is "infinite absolute negativity": it negates the whole given actuality of Athens without erecting a positive doctrine, and thereby becomes the midwife of individual subjectivity. The dissertation also criticizes German Romantic irony for remaining in that negativity, and argues irony must be "mastered" — made a controlled moment in an earnest existence. Users routinely confuse the title with The Concept of Anxiety (1844, Vigilius Haufniensis), a different book on a different subject.

**Citations:**
- On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates (1841)

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Your dissertation On the Concept of Anxiety…" is a standard title-mashup; the correct behavior is to fix the title before answering.

**Provenance:** SEP §1; IEP §1c, §2a. Motivated by stress question kg-22 (passed with zero corpus support — the work is absent from the entire corpus, works index included).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Ultimatum: Either/Or's Final Word Belongs to a Country Parson

**Claim:**

Either/Or ends with the "Ultimatum" — a sermon by an anonymous Jutland pastor on the theme that in relation to God we are always in the wrong.

**Explanation:**

The book's last word belongs neither to the aesthete "A" nor to Judge William. The Judge forwards to his young friend a sermon composed by a pastor friend in Jutland: "The Upbuilding That Lies in the Thought That in Relation to God We Are Always in the Wrong." Against every legal, calculating relation to God — where one might be "in the right" — the sermon finds rest and freedom precisely in being always in the wrong before God, as love wishes to be. Structurally it points beyond both life-views of the book toward the religious, and it anticipates the upbuilding literature Kierkegaard published under his own name.

**Citations:**
- Either/Or, Part II, "Ultimatum" (1843)

**Provenance:** IEP §2a; SEP §4 (citing EO II 354 nearby). Motivated by stress question kg-10 (passed with zero corpus support).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Discourse vs. Deliberation — and Which Works of Love Is

**Claim:**

Kierkegaard distinguished "upbuilding discourses," which presuppose and build on the reader's Christian concepts, from "deliberations," which must first unsettle and awaken — and Works of Love is deliberations.

**Explanation:**

By Kierkegaard's own explanation in his journals, an upbuilding discourse (Tale) assumes people know what love is and aims to win them over to it; a deliberation (Overveielse) must first disturb a comfortable way of thinking — it aims to awaken, provoke, and sharpen thought. Works of Love is subtitled "Some Christian Deliberations in the Form of Discourses": its topsy-turvy, polemical weighing of the command "You shall love" is deliberation-work, not consolation. Calling Works of Love a "discourse" flattens a distinction Kierkegaard drew deliberately.

**Citations:**
- Works of Love (1847), subtitle and structure
- Journals (Kierkegaard's explanation of Tale vs. Overveielse)

**Misreadings (optional):**

The current works index line for Works of Love says "signed religious discourse" — that should be corrected to "deliberations" when this card is adopted.

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (discourse/deliberation paragraph); IEP §3a. Motivated by stress question kg-30 (passed — the system corrected the corpus's own index; protect that behavior).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Postscript Was Meant to End the Authorship

**Claim:**

Kierkegaard intended Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846) to conclude his authorship, after which he planned to become a country pastor.

**Explanation:**

The "concluding" in the title was meant literally: the Postscript surveys and closes the pseudonymous production, and Kierkegaard appended "A First and Last Explanation" acknowledging the pseudonyms under his own name. He imagined retiring to a rural parsonage. The Corsair affair intervened: made reclusive by public mockery, he came to feel called to remain at his post as a writer, and the second authorship followed — Works of Love, Christian Discourses, The Sickness unto Death, Practice in Christianity, and finally the attack literature. Without this card, "did you mean to stop writing?" has no corpus grounding at all.

**Citations:**
- Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846), incl. "A First and Last Explanation"

**Provenance:** SEP §1; IEP §1d–e. Motivated by stress questions kg-35 (passed with zero corpus support), kg-04.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Revocation at the End of the Postscript

**Claim:**

Johannes Climacus ends the Concluding Unscientific Postscript by revoking everything the book has said, while insisting that to revoke something is not the same as never having said it.

**Explanation:**

Climacus is a self-professed humorist, not a Christian. Having argued that essential truth is a matter of inward appropriation rather than objective results, he cannot leave his own book standing as one more objective result to be pocketed — so he takes it back. The revocation is the consummation of indirect communication, not a confession of error: the reader is returned empty-handed to the task of existing. Readings that treat the revocation as a joke that cancels the book, or ignore it entirely, both miss its function.

**Citations:**
- Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846), "An Understanding with the Reader" (the revocation)

**Provenance:** IEP §2d; SEP §4 (the revocation debates). Motivated by stress question kg-33 (passed with zero corpus support).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Anti-Climacus vs. Johannes Climacus: The Standpoint Hierarchy

**Claim:**

Anti-Climacus writes from an idealized Christian standpoint above Kierkegaard himself, whereas Johannes Climacus is a humorist who claims not to be a Christian; Kierkegaard placed himself between the two.

**Explanation:**

The "Anti-" marks contrast of standpoint, not hostility. Climacus (Philosophical Fragments, Postscript) circles the difficulty of becoming a Christian from outside; Anti-Climacus (The Sickness Unto Death, Practice in Christianity) speaks from the full ideality of Christian faith. Kierkegaard signed himself only as editor of the Anti-Climacus books because he would not claim that elevation for his own life: lodging the requirement in a pseudonym above himself kept him, too, under its judgment. Scholars accordingly treat Anti-Climacus as only "weakly" pseudonymous — the closest thing to Kierkegaard's highest voice.

**Citations:**
- The Sickness Unto Death (1849) and Practice in Christianity (1850), title pages (Anti-Climacus, "edited by S. Kierkegaard")
- Journals (Kierkegaard on placing himself between Climacus and Anti-Climacus)

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (Anti-Climacus paragraph); IEP §1e, §4b. Motivated by stress question kg-36 (passed with retrieval returning zero sources).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Repetition: Published the Same Day as Fear and Trembling

**Claim:**

Repetition (by Constantin Constantius) was published on the same day in October 1843 as Fear and Trembling, and defines repetition as "recollection forwards."

**Explanation:**

The two books appeared simultaneously, not in sequence — a fact users misstate and the corpus nowhere records. Constantin Constantius distinguishes Platonic recollection (a backward movement toward a truth already possessed) from repetition, the forward movement in which what is lost is received again — the definitive movement of the religious life. The book stages failed repetitions: Constantius cannot repeat his Berlin journey, and the "young man" gets no further than religious poetry, turning out to be Constantius's invention.

**Citations:**
- Repetition (1843, Constantin Constantius)

**Provenance:** SEP Chronology 1843 ("Published the same day as Fear and Trembling"); IEP §2b. Motivated by stress question kg-24 (passed with only partial corpus support).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The "Blond Beast" Is the Lion of All Conquering Aristocracies, Not a Germanic Racial Ideal

**Claim:** Nietzsche's "blond beast" names the lion-like predatory core he ascribes to every ancient ruling caste, expressly including non-Germanic nobilities.

**Explanation:** In the Genealogy's First Essay, Nietzsche describes the "magnificent blonde brute, avidly rampant for spoil and victory" at the core of all aristocratic races, and his own list runs: Roman, Arabic, German, and Japanese nobility, the Homeric heroes, the Scandinavian Vikings. The blondness belongs to the lion image, not to hair color or race — the same section mocks the idea of continuity between the old Germanic tribes and modern Germans ("scarcely a psychological, let alone a physical, relationship"). The passage is diagnosis of a type, not praise of a nation, and it directly blocks the Nazi-era racial reading.

**Citations:**
- On the Genealogy of Morality, First Essay §11

**Misreadings (optional):** "Blond beast = Aryan/Germanic master race" — refuted by the multi-ethnic list inside the very passage; also inconsistent with Nietzsche's anti-nationalist polemics.

**Provenance:** GM I 11 verified in Samuel translation (Gutenberg #52319); IEP "Nietzsche" §8 (reception/distortion). Motivated by stress-test question nz-27 (no corpus item mentioned the blond beast; the baseline persona answered correctly from model knowledge with no grounding).

### The "Higher Nature" Today Is a Battleground of Both Moralities

**Claim:** Nietzsche says modern individuals — especially higher ones — contain both the good/bad and good/evil valuations in tension, and modern moralities are mixtures of the two.

**Explanation:** The Genealogy's First Essay does not end by telling readers to pick master morality. Its closing section says the two opposed value-schemes have fought for millennia, that today there is "perhaps no more decisive mark of the higher nature" than to be a genuine battleground of these opposites, and Beyond Good and Evil adds that in all modern moralities the two types interpenetrate and mix, even within a single soul. This is the standard corrective to the misreading that Nietzsche simply advocates trampling the weak or reenacting archaic nobility: his positive project is new value creation, not historical reenactment — while his anti-egalitarianism remains genuine.

**Citations:**
- On the Genealogy of Morality, First Essay §16
- Beyond Good and Evil §260

**Misreadings (optional):** "Praising master morality = endorsing today's powerful / a program of cruelty" — the battleground and mixture passages block the inference.

**Provenance:** SEP "Nietzsche" §2 (GM I 16 quoted) and §3.2.1 (against sanitized and brutalized readings alike). Motivated by stress-test question nz-28 (check nz-28-c3 failed: the baseline reply made the diagnosis-not-prescription point but had no access to GM I 16 / BGE 260).

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Pathos of Distance: Where Noble Valuation Comes From

**Claim:** The "pathos of distance" is the ruling order's enduring felt superiority over the ruled, out of which Nietzsche says the right to create values first arose.

**Explanation:** In the Genealogy's First Essay the nobles' good/bad valuation does not begin from utility or altruism but from the lasting, felt interval between a ruling caste and those below it; from that height the nobles seized the right to coin names and call their own being "good." Beyond Good and Evil §257 extends the idea inward: out of the social distance grows the demand for ever-greater "distance within the soul itself" — self-overcoming and rank among one's own states. The concept therefore links the historical origin story of values to Nietzsche's positive ideal of self-mastery.

**Citations:**
- On the Genealogy of Morality, First Essay §2
- Beyond Good and Evil §257

**Provenance:** SEP "Nietzsche" §2 (GM I 2, pathos of distance). Motivated by stress-test question nz-32 (content answered correctly; both citations omitted — check nz-32-c2). Currently the phrase appears only inside the "Master Morality" card without the BGE §257 locus.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Psychology as "Queen of the Sciences" (BGE 23)

**Claim:** Nietzsche closes Part I of Beyond Good and Evil demanding that psychology — as "morphology and doctrine of development of the will to power" — again be recognized as queen of the sciences.

**Explanation:** BGE §23 argues that all psychology so far has been moralized psychology, afraid of its own depths; a psychology that dares to treat the affects philosophers condemn (hatred, envy, lust to rule) as conditions of life becomes the path back to the fundamental problems, and the other sciences exist for its service and preparation. The passage matters twice over: it is the clearest published statement of what the will to power is supposed to do inside Nietzsche's philosophy (a psychological research program, not a political slogan), and it is a standard "where does he say X?" locate-question.

**Citations:**
- Beyond Good and Evil §23

**Provenance:** SEP "Nietzsche" §4 (quoting BGE 23); text verified against ingested chunk bge-023 (Zimmern). Motivated by stress-test question nz-09 (answered correctly with empty retrieval; no card or index entry exists for this locus).

### Wagner: Devotion in 1872, Break by 1878, Polemics in 1888

**Claim:** The Birth of Tragedy championed Wagner; the friendship broke around 1876–1878 (Bayreuth, then Human, All Too Human), and the anti-Wagner polemics date from 1888.

**Explanation:** Nietzsche's first book (1872) presented Wagnerian music-drama as the possible rebirth of tragic culture and was written inside the friendship. Disillusionment set in around the first Bayreuth festival (1876), and sending Wagner Human, All Too Human (1878) ended the relationship — Nietzsche later said the book and the Parsifal libretto crossed in the mail "as if two swords had crossed." The explicit polemics, The Case of Wagner and Nietzsche contra Wagner, come only in 1888. Getting this order right blocks a common reversal (reading The Birth of Tragedy as an attack on Wagner) and anchors the early/middle/late periodization of his work.

**Citations:**
- The Birth of Tragedy (1872), dedication and §§16–25 (Wagner as hope of tragic rebirth)
- Ecce Homo, "Human, All-too-Human" §5 ("two swords" remark; approximate locator)
- The Case of Wagner (1888)

**Provenance:** SEP "Nietzsche" §1 Life and Works; IEP "Nietzsche" §1. Motivated by stress-test question nz-24 (answered correctly from model knowledge; the corpus holds only two one-line works-index entries and has no biography/Wagner card).

### Emotions as Magical Transformations of the World

**Claim:** In his Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions (1939), Sartre argues an emotion is an active, "magical" transformation of how consciousness grasps a difficult situation, not a passive state we suffer.

**Explanation:** When the world's ordinary instrumental paths are blocked — the danger cannot be escaped, the obstacle cannot be moved — consciousness changes the world the only way left: by changing its own way of grasping it. Fainting abolishes the terrifying object by abolishing my consciousness of it; rage treats an unmovable obstacle as if striking it could remove it. Because this transformation is conduct of consciousness rather than something merely undergone, we bear responsibility even for our emotions. The account extends Sartre's early phenomenological psychology and points forward to bad faith: treating my emotion as a thing that "happened to me" is one more way of fleeing freedom.

**Citations:**
- Sketch for a Theory of the Emotions (Esquisse d'une théorie des émotions, 1939) — the "magical transformation" analysis (location approximate)

**Misreadings (optional):** Sartre does not claim we consciously "decide" to be afraid; the transformation is spontaneous and pre-reflective, which is precisely why it can be sincere and still ours.

**Provenance:** IEP §2a (magical transformation, fainting, wrath examples); SEP §3 (The Emotions: Outline of a Theory, 1939, "world-shaping powers of emotions"). Motivating question: sartre-q07. Also add the Sketch (1939) to the works index — it is currently absent.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Search for a Method and the Progressive-Regressive Method

**Claim:** In Search for a Method (1957, attached to the Critique of Dialectical Reason), Sartre proposes a progressive-regressive method combining Marxist analysis of conditions with existential reconstruction of the individual project.

**Explanation:** Sartre thought postwar Marxism had frozen into a scholasticism that dissolved individuals into abstractions — his shorthand: Valéry is a petit-bourgeois intellectual, but not every petit-bourgeois intellectual is Valéry. The regressive moment works backward from a life to the material, class, and historical conditions that shaped it; the progressive moment reconstructs how the person surpassed those conditions toward a singular project. The method is Sartre's bridge between existential psychoanalysis and historical materialism, and its great test case is the Flaubert study, The Family Idiot.

**Citations:**
- Search for a Method (Questions de méthode, 1957; prefixed to Critique of Dialectical Reason, 1960)
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §§5–6 (progressive-regressive method; Valéry passage, Sartre 1957 [1963: 56]); IEP §7b (The Problem of Method). Motivating question: sartre-q13. Also add Search for a Method (1957) to the works index.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Family Idiot: The Flaubert Project

**Claim:** The Family Idiot (1971–72) is Sartre's massive unfinished study of Gustave Flaubert, uniting existential psychoanalysis with Marxist history to ask what we can know of one man.

**Explanation:** Sartre's last great work applies the progressive-regressive method to a single life: how the "unloved child" and supposed family idiot made himself, out of what his family, class, and era made of him, into the author of Madame Bovary. It synthesizes the two halves of Sartre's career — the fundamental project of Being and Nothingness and the social-historical mediations of the Critique. Sartre counted it among the works he was most proud of. It ran to several volumes and remained incomplete.

**Citations:**
- The Family Idiot (L'Idiot de la famille, 1971–72) — location approximate

**Misreadings (optional):** Sometimes confused with a study of Proust or of Genet; the subject is Flaubert (Saint Genet, 1952, is the Genet study).

**Provenance:** SEP intro and §5 (Flaubert, "the author of Madame Bovary", 1971–2); IEP §1. Motivating questions: sartre-q24, sartre-q13. Also add The Family Idiot to the works index.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Break with Camus (1952)

**Claim:** Sartre and Camus fell out publicly in 1952 after Camus' The Rebel received a hostile review in Sartre's journal Les Temps Modernes, not over the "existentialist" label.

**Explanation:** The Rebel (1951) criticized revolutionary violence and the Marxist justification of historical crimes; Francis Jeanson's harsh review in Les Temps Modernes and the ensuing exchange of open letters — including Sartre's own reply to Camus — ended the friendship. The disagreement was political and philosophical: Camus urged limits on rebellion, while Sartre held one could not stand outside history with clean hands. Camus' long-standing refusal of the existentialist label was never the cause of the rupture. The "absurd" is Camus' signature concept, not Sartre's.

**Citations:**
- Les Temps Modernes exchange, 1952 (Jeanson's review of The Rebel; Sartre, "Réponse à Albert Camus") — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (reception of The Rebel in Les Temps Modernes 1951–2 "caused Sartre and Camus to very publicly fall out", citing Aronson 2004). Motivating questions: sartre-q19, sartre-q17.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Heidegger's "Letter on Humanism" Against Sartre

**Claim:** Heidegger's "Letter on Humanism" (1946–47) rejected Sartre's Existentialism Is a Humanism, arguing that "existence precedes essence" merely inverts a metaphysical thesis and misses the question of Being.

**Explanation:** Far from endorsing Sartre's reading of Being and Time, Heidegger used the Letter to distance himself from "existentialism" and from humanism as one more metaphysical position. Reversing the priority of essence and existence, he argued, keeps the old metaphysical framework intact; the analysis of Dasein was only preparatory to the deeper question of the meaning of Being, of which man is the "shepherd," not the center. The episode marks the sharpest philosophical divide between the two thinkers whom the public most often lumped together.

**Citations:**
- Heidegger, "Letter on Humanism" (1946/47) — response to Existentialism Is a Humanism

**Provenance:** IEP §2d (Heidegger's Letter: Sartre's humanism as "one more metaphysical perspective which does not return to the deeper issue of the meaning of Being"). Motivating question: sartre-q20.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Promised Ethics: A Footnote, the Notebooks, and Beauvoir

**Claim:** Being and Nothingness ends with a footnote promising an ethics Sartre never published; his Notebooks for an Ethics appeared posthumously, and The Ethics of Ambiguity is Beauvoir's book, not his.

**Explanation:** Being and Nothingness offers no direct account of good faith; its closing pages promise a future work on ethics that never appeared in Sartre's lifetime. His drafts were published posthumously as Notebooks for an Ethics (1983), and sustained treatments of authenticity appear instead in Anti-Semite and Jew. Simone de Beauvoir's The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947) is the era's worked-out existentialist ethics, drawing consequences from the shared ontology — it is routinely misattributed to Sartre and should not be. This card exists chiefly to block that misattribution and to explain why "Sartre's ethics" is a reconstruction.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness (1943), concluding footnote promising an ethics
- Notebooks for an Ethics (posthumous, 1983) — location approximate

**Misreadings (optional):** Attributing The Ethics of Ambiguity to Sartre; assuming Existentialism Is a Humanism is the promised ethics.

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 ("the enigmatic footnote at the end of the book that promises an ethics"; Notebooks for an Ethics; authenticity in Anti-Semite and Jew); SEP §4 (The Ethics of Ambiguity as Beauvoir's). Motivating questions: sartre-q21, sartre-q29.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Fellow Traveler, Never a Party Member

**Claim:** Sartre was never a member of the French Communist Party; he was for a period a close fellow traveler and publicly condemned the Soviet invasion of Hungary in 1956.

**Explanation:** Sartre's Marxism was always unaffiliated: he judged historical materialism the only valid interpretation of history while insisting existentialism remained the only concrete approach to reality, and he refused Party discipline. In the early 1950s he aligned closely with the PCF as a fellow traveler; the Soviet crushing of the 1956 Hungarian rising drew his public condemnation and ended the alignment. He also condemned the invasion of Czechoslovakia (1968) and by 1977 said he was no longer a Marxist. Questions premised on his "Party membership" rest on a false fact.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — Sartre's self-positioning toward Marxism (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §6 ("Never a member of the French Communist Party..."); IEP §1 (condemning the USSR's invasions of Hungary and Czechoslovakia; 1977 no-longer-Marxist claim). Motivating question: sartre-q22.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Apricot Cocktail: Aron and the Discovery of Phenomenology

**Claim:** Around 1932–33 Raymond Aron introduced Sartre to phenomenology in a Paris bar, using an apricot cocktail as the example; Sartre then spent a year in Berlin studying Husserl.

**Explanation:** As Beauvoir tells it, Aron — back from Berlin — gestured at his glass and said one could make philosophy out of this very cocktail. The promise of a rigorous philosophy of the concrete electrified Sartre, who read Levinas on Husserl and left to study in Berlin (1933–34), where he drafted what became Transcendence of the Ego and the intentionality essay. The interlocutor was Aron, not Merleau-Ponty — a common confusion worth blocking. The anecdote anchors the chronology of Sartre's whole phenomenological turn.

**Citations:**
- Beauvoir's memoirs (The Prime of Life) recounting the scene — location approximate

**Misreadings (optional):** Crediting Merleau-Ponty (or Husserl in person) with the introduction; dating the discovery after Being and Nothingness.

**Provenance:** SEP §2 ("in a Parisian bar listening to his friend Raymond Aron's description of an apricot cocktail", turn of 1932/33; Berlin year; Levinas); IEP §1 (Berlin 1933). Motivating question: sartre-q34.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Quasi-Observation Thesis: Why Imagining Teaches Nothing New

**Claim:** In The Imaginary (1940), Sartre argues the imagined object contains only what consciousness puts into it, so imagining, unlike perceiving, can never teach us anything new.

**Explanation:** A perceived object is inexhaustible — it always has further sides and details to discover, and can surprise me. An imagined object is built entirely from knowledge I already have; it exhibits an "essential poverty," yielding no observation and no surprise. Sartre pairs this with the claim that the image posits its object as absent, non-existent, or unreal — the image "wraps a certain nothingness" — and concludes that the power to irrealize is the very freedom of consciousness: a being wholly engulfed in the real could never imagine. This is the doctrinal core missing behind the works-index one-liner on The Imaginary.

**Citations:**
- The Imaginary (1940) — quasi-observation thesis and the conclusion on imagination and freedom (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §3 (quasi-observation thesis; image gives its object "as not being"; "For consciousness to be able to imagine ... it must be free", Sartre 1940 [2004: 184]). Motivating question: sartre-q31.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### Black Orpheus and Fanon's Critique

**Claim:** Sartre's "Black Orpheus" (1948) cast negritude as an "anti-racist racism" — a dialectical stage toward a raceless struggle — and Fanon's Black Skin, White Masks (1952) criticized this as erasing lived black experience.

**Explanation:** Prefacing the 1948 anthology of negritude poetry, Sartre declared himself on the movement's side while framing negritude as a moment destined to be surpassed in a universal (class) struggle. Fanon objected that this turned a lived, present consciousness into a mere transitional term, undermining its power from a position outside it. The two later effected a rapprochement, culminating in Sartre's 1961 preface to Fanon's The Wretched of the Earth, where Sartre appears to endorse anti-colonial counter-violence. The corpus currently records the Wretched preface but not the earlier essay or the criticism.

**Citations:**
- "Black Orpheus" (1948), preface to the negritude anthology
- Fanon, Black Skin, White Masks (1952); Sartre's preface to The Wretched of the Earth (1961)

**Provenance:** SEP §7 (Black Orpheus, "anti-racist racism", Fanon's response, the 1961 preface). Motivating question: sartre-q32.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

### The Desire to Be God

**Claim:** In Being and Nothingness, Sartre argues the for-itself's fundamental desire is to become an impossible in-itself-for-itself — to be its own foundation — which is what "God" names.

**Explanation:** The for-itself is a lack that seeks the solidity and self-identity of the in-itself without surrendering its own translucent freedom: to be causa sui, ground of its own being. That synthesis is contradictory, so the project structurally fails — hence Sartre's famous verdict that "man is a useless passion." The doctrine anchors his account of desire (all desiring-to-have reduces to desiring-to-be), of love's impossible demand, and of why no human life can reach final self-coincidence. Currently only a note on the "useless passion" quote carries this; it deserves a card.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness (1943), Part Four — the fundamental project and desire for being (location approximate)

**Provenance:** IEP §4c (desire for being, "to become God", three unrealizable forms); SEP §5 (fundamental project); corpus quote note on "Man is a useless passion". Motivating question: sartre-q10.

**Status:** draft (stress-test proposal)

## A. Existence Precedes Essence

### Existence Precedes Essence

**Claim:** For Sartre, a human being first simply exists, and only later, through living, defines what they are — there is no pre-set human essence.

**Explanation:** For a manufactured thing like a knife, the idea of it (what it's *for*) comes before the actual object — someone designs a knife to cut, then makes it. Sartre says human beings are the opposite: we show up in the world first, with no blueprint, and only afterward, through the choices we make, do we become anything in particular — kind, cowardly, an artist, a parent. There's no design spec for a person handed down in advance. This is Sartre's single most famous slogan, and it's the foundation everything else in his philosophy is built on.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism
- Being and Nothingness, Introduction and Part One (location approximate — the phrase itself is most closely associated with the 1945 lecture)

**Misreadings (optional):** People sometimes hear this as "there's no such thing as human nature so nothing about being human is stable or shared" — but Sartre still thinks there's a common human condition (see the separate card on that distinction); what he denies is a fixed essence or purpose given in advance.

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (Life and Works, mentions the 1945 lecture); IEP §2d (Existential Phenomenology), which states the "existence precedes essence" formula directly.

**Status:** draft

### The Paper-Knife Contrast: Why Humans Differ from Artifacts

**Claim:** Sartre argues humans differ from manufactured objects because no one designed us with a purpose before we existed.

**Explanation:** Think about a letter-opener: a craftsman had an idea of "a thing for opening letters" in mind before cutting the metal — the concept (essence) precedes the object (existence). Sartre says that if there were a God who made people the way a craftsman makes a letter-opener, then human nature would precede human existence the same way. But Sartre argues there is no such designer, so nothing about our purpose is fixed in advance. We exist first, and only by acting do we give ourselves a nature — bit by bit, choice by choice, rather than all at once by design.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate — this is the lecture's opening illustration)

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the lecture's opening argument, cross-checked against SEP §1 and IEP §2d for the existence-precedes-essence formula this illustration supports.

**Status:** draft

### No Human Nature, But a Shared Human Condition

**Claim:** Sartre denies a fixed human "nature" but still affirms a universal human "condition" — basic limits every person faces.

**Explanation:** This distinction gets lost a lot. Sartre isn't saying human beings have nothing in common — he's saying nothing is handed to us as a finished character or purpose. What we do all share are certain unavoidable facts of being human: we're born into a body, we live among other people, we have to work to survive, and we will die. Sartre calls this shared backdrop the "human condition" rather than a "human nature," because these are limits we all face, not a script telling us who to be within them. How each person responds to these limits is still entirely up to them.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)

**Provenance:** IEP §2d and §4a on the for-itself having no fixed identity, generalized to the nature/condition distinction Sartre draws in the 1945 lecture.

**Status:** draft

### Contingency: Existence Has No Built-In Reason

**Claim:** In his novel *Nausea*, Sartre dramatizes the idea that things (and people) simply exist, with no built-in justification for why they're there at all.

**Explanation:** In the novel, the character Roquentin stares at the root of a chestnut tree and is overwhelmed by the sense that it's just *there* — bulging, black, absurdly present — with no reason it has to look that way or exist at all. This "nausea" is Sartre's vivid, pre-philosophical way of showing what he later argues technically: existence isn't logically necessary or justified by some plan. Things (and by extension, our own lives) don't come with a built-in "why." That shock is uncomfortable, but for Sartre it's also the doorway to real freedom, because if nothing about us is fixed by necessity, nothing stops us from making something of ourselves.

**Citations:**
- Nausea (location approximate — the well-known chestnut-tree-root scene, roughly two-thirds through the novel)

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (Life and Works), which lists *Nausea* (1938) as a major early work; general background on the novel's central scene, cross-checked for consistency with the ontology described in SEP §4.1.

**Status:** draft

### Subjectivity as the Starting Point

**Claim:** Sartre holds that any philosophy of human existence must start from the first-person experience of an individual choosing, not from abstract theories about "man" in general.

**Explanation:** Instead of starting with a theory of what humans are supposed to be — a "nature," a divine plan, a scientific definition — Sartre insists philosophy has to start where each of us actually stands: an individual person, in a situation, making a choice, right now. He borrows this starting point from Descartes's famous "I think, therefore I am," but modernizes it into something less about abstract certainty and more about lived, active existence. Only after starting there, Sartre argues, can you build any honest picture of human life — everything else (values, meaning, identity) gets constructed outward from that point, not handed down onto it.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate — Sartre discusses this near the lecture's opening, invoking the Cartesian cogito)

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the lecture's structure; cross-checked against IEP §2b/§2d discussion of pre-reflective vs. reflective consciousness as the basic starting point of Sartre's method.

**Status:** draft

## B. Radical Freedom and Responsibility

### Freedom Is Built Into Consciousness Itself

**Claim:** For Sartre, freedom isn't a special extra power some people have more of — it's simply what it is to be a conscious being at all.

**Explanation:** Sartre argues that the very fact you can question things, imagine alternatives, and step back from your immediate situation shows that your mind isn't just another object bumping along according to fixed causes, the way a rock or a chemical reaction is. Any time you ask "what if things were different?" you've already mentally detached yourself from how things actually are — and that small act of detachment is what Sartre calls freedom. It isn't something you have to earn or achieve; it's built into the basic structure of having a mind that's always reaching toward what isn't yet the case.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 ("The Origin of Negation")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (Negation and freedom) — discussion of questioning, negation, and freedom as "ontologically basic"; IEP §3c (Nothingness).

**Status:** draft

---

### "Condemned to Be Free"

**Claim:** Sartre's phrase "condemned to be free" means we cannot escape making choices — even refusing to choose is itself a choice.

**Explanation:** You might think you could dodge responsibility by just not deciding anything — going with the flow, following orders, doing what's expected. Sartre says that's an illusion: "going along with it" is itself something you're doing, a choice you're making, whether you admit it to yourself or not. There's no neutral position outside of choosing. That's why he calls freedom a kind of sentence rather than a gift — you didn't ask to be born into a situation where you have to keep choosing every moment of your life, but there it is, and there's no opting out of it.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Four, Ch. 1 ("Being and Doing: Freedom") — location approximate
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)

**Provenance:** IEP §6a (Freedom) — "Sartre presents his notion of freedom as amounting to making choices, and indeed not being able to avoid making choices."

**Status:** draft

---

### Freedom Operates Within Facticity, Not Apart from It

**Claim:** Sartre's freedom is always freedom *in a situation* — bound up with the concrete facts of your body, history, and circumstances, which he calls facticity.

**Explanation:** "Facticity" is just Sartre's word for all the stuff about your life you didn't choose and can't undo: your body, your past, your nationality, the era you were born into, the fact that you're tired right now. Sartre never denies any of this is real or that it limits you. His point is narrower: none of these facts, by themselves, tells you what they *mean* or what to do about them. A steep mountain trail is only "too hard" once you decide to treat your exhaustion as a reason to stop rather than push through — the fatigue is a fact, but how it counts for you is something you bring to it.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 — the hiker/fatigue example illustrating freedom operating "within facticity."

**Status:** draft

---

### Radical Freedom Does Not Mean Unconstrained by Circumstances

**Claim:** Sartre's claim that we are radically free is not the claim that circumstances don't limit us — it's a claim about how we relate to whatever limits we face.

**Explanation:** This is probably the most common misunderstanding of Sartre. "Radical freedom" sounds like it should mean nothing can hold you back — but Sartre never says that, and it would be a strange thing for a philosopher who lived through Nazi occupation to say. What he actually argues is more modest and, honestly, more interesting: your circumstances (poverty, illness, a hostile society) don't come with a built-in verdict about what they mean or demand of you. Two people can face the identical hardship and take it up completely differently — as an excuse, as a challenge, as a tragedy to endure. That gap between the bare fact and what you do with it is where Sartre locates freedom, not in some fantasy of being unconstrained.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 (location approximate)

**Misreadings (optional):** Treating "radical freedom" as meaning you can simply will yourself out of poverty, illness, or oppression by choosing differently — Sartre explicitly builds facticity (the constraints you didn't choose) into his account of freedom rather than denying it.

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (situated freedom, facticity); IEP §6a (freedom as "characteristic of the nature of consciousness," not an ability to do anything whatsoever).

**Status:** draft

---

### Radical Responsibility: No One to Blame

**Claim:** Because there is no fixed human nature or external authority dictating our choices, Sartre holds that each person bears total responsibility for who they become.

**Explanation:** If there were a pre-set human nature, or a God handing down rules, you could always point somewhere else when things went wrong — "that's just how I am," or "I was only following the rules." Sartre closes off both exits. Since there's no essence you're just expressing and no authority whose orders excuse you, whatever you do is authored by you, full stop. This is a heavier burden than it sounds: it means you can't outsource blame (or credit) to your upbringing, your psychology, or your circumstances, even though all of those genuinely shaped the situation you're choosing within.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)
- Being and Nothingness, Part Four (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (freedom as constitutive of human reality); IEP §2c (Ethics) — consciousness as spontaneous and transparent, so the agent is "fully responsible."

**Status:** draft

### In Choosing for Yourself, You Choose an Image of Humanity

**Claim:** Sartre argues that every individual choice implicitly endorses a value that we'd want everyone in a similar situation to act on.

**Explanation:** When you choose to do something, Sartre says you're not just picking an action for yourself in isolation — you're implicitly saying "this is a good way for a person to act," which commits you to thinking it would be fine for anyone else in your position to act the same way. If you decide honesty matters and lie anyway, you're quietly claiming lying is fine for people generally, not just convenient for you right now. So even though Sartre denies there are ready-made universal values sitting out there waiting to be discovered, he thinks individual choices still carry a kind of universal weight — they're personal, but they're never *merely* personal.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)

**Provenance:** IEP §6c (An Ethical Dimension) — "By choosing, an individual commits not only himself, but the whole of humanity" and the discussion of the "singular universal."

**Status:** draft

---

### Determinism as an Alibi

**Claim:** Sartre treats appeals to psychological or social determinism ("I couldn't help it, that's just how I was raised") as a disguised attempt to escape responsibility, not a neutral scientific fact.

**Explanation:** People often explain their behavior by pointing to causes outside their control — their upbringing, their unconscious drives, their social class — as if that settles the matter and lets them off the hook. Sartre doesn't deny these factors are real influences, but he argues that reaching for them *as an excuse* is itself a choice, and often a self-deceiving one. Nothing forces you to let your past dictate your next move; treating your history as destiny is something you're doing right now, in the present, to avoid facing the fact that you're still choosing.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 ("Bad Faith")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 — Sartre's critique of psychoanalytic determinism and "naturalist assumptions"; IEP §2c (Ethics) on rejecting excuses grounded in unconscious forces.

**Status:** draft

## C. Bad Faith (Mauvaise Foi)

### Bad Faith Defined: Lying to Yourself, Not Others

**Claim:** Bad faith is Sartre's term for a specific kind of self-deception in which a person hides the truth from themselves while somehow still knowing it.

**Explanation:** Ordinary lying involves two people: someone who knows the truth and someone being deceived. Bad faith is stranger, because the deceiver and the deceived are the same person. Somehow you manage to hide something from yourself that, on some level, you already know — like telling yourself you "have no choice" about a job you're actually too comfortable to leave. Sartre insists this only makes sense because consciousness isn't a simple, unified thing sealed off in layers (like a "conscious mind" hiding things from an "unconscious mind") — it's one continuous awareness that can, strangely, look away from itself.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 ("Bad Faith")

**Misreadings (optional):** Bad faith is not the same thing as ordinary lying to other people — it's a self-directed structure of self-deception, which is why Sartre thinks it needs its own special explanation.

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 (Bad faith and the critique of Freudian psychoanalysis).

**Status:** draft

---

### The Café Waiter

**Claim:** Sartre's example of a waiter who overacts the role of "waiter" illustrates bad faith as pretending you simply *are* your social role, with no freedom left over.

**Explanation:** Picture a waiter whose every movement is a little too precise, a little too eager — he's not just doing the job, he's performing "Being a Waiter" as though it were his entire identity, like an object that couldn't be anything else. Sartre's point isn't that having a job or playing a social role is bad faith in itself — we all have roles. The bad faith is in pretending you're *nothing but* the role, that you have no more freedom or choice than a cup has about being a cup, when in fact you could always, at any moment, do otherwise. It's a way of hiding from your own freedom by dressing up as a thing.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 ("Bad Faith")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 (the café waiter example); IEP §4b (The Project of Bad Faith).

**Status:** draft

---

### Bad Faith by Collapsing into a Thing

**Claim:** One pattern of bad faith is denying your own freedom by acting as though you were a fixed object rather than an open-ended, choosing being.

**Explanation:** Sartre says human beings are always partly "facticity" (the given facts about you) and partly "transcendence" (your ongoing freedom to go beyond those facts and choose what they mean). One way to fall into bad faith is to collapse entirely into the facts and pretend that's all there is to you — "I'm just an anxious person," "I'm just not the kind of person who speaks up," said not as an observation but as a way of foreclosing any further choice. It's comforting because it makes decisions feel unnecessary — but for Sartre it's a denial of something real about yourself: that you're always more than whatever you've been so far.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 — bad faith as "exclusive affirmation" of facticity or transcendence.

**Status:** draft

---

### Bad Faith by Denying Your Situation

**Claim:** The opposite pattern of bad faith is treating yourself as pure limitless possibility, refusing to acknowledge the concrete facts and commitments already shaping your situation.

**Explanation:** Sartre's famous example is a woman on a date who lets a man hold her hand but mentally treats it as if it isn't really happening — a fact she doesn't have to register or respond to, as though she were pure, unattached possibility floating above the situation. This is bad faith running the other direction from "collapsing into a thing": here, someone denies the real facts and commitments already in play (that the hand-holding is happening, that it means something, that a response is called for) by retreating into an imagined freedom disconnected from any actual situation. Both patterns dodge the same uncomfortable truth: that we are always both fact and freedom at once, and have to own both.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 (location approximate — the "woman on a first date" example)

**Provenance:** General knowledge of Sartre's second worked example in the bad faith chapter, cross-checked against SEP §4.2's description of bad faith as oscillation between facticity and transcendence.

**Status:** draft

---

### Why Bad Faith Is Self-Undermining

**Claim:** Sartre argues bad faith is inherently unstable because the very freedom it tries to deny is what makes the denial possible in the first place.

**Explanation:** There's a built-in irony to bad faith: to convince yourself you have no choice, you have to actively, freely work at not-noticing your choices — which is itself an exercise of the freedom you're claiming not to have. It's a bit like someone straining very hard to convince themselves they aren't trying. Because the deception depends on the very freedom it denies, Sartre says bad faith always carries "the seed of its own destruction" — the truth stays available, half-visible, ready to be noticed the moment you stop working so hard not to see it.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 ("bad faith... always contains the germ of its destruction"); IEP §4b ("The freedom of the for-itself is a pre-condition for the project of bad faith which denies it").

**Status:** draft

---

### Sartre's Critique of Freud's Unconscious

**Claim:** Sartre rejects Freud's unconscious mind as an explanation of self-deception, arguing it just relocates the puzzle instead of solving it.

**Explanation:** Freud explains how you can deceive yourself by splitting the mind into parts — an unconscious part that knows the uncomfortable truth, and a conscious part that's shielded from it by an internal "censor." Sartre's objection: that censor itself has to somehow know exactly what to hide in order to hide it effectively, while also somehow not-knowing it, which just recreates the very paradox (knowing and not-knowing at once) that Freud was trying to explain — now happening inside one internal referee instead of inside the whole person. Sartre thinks this doesn't solve self-deception, it just hides an extra little person inside your head who has the same problem you started with.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 ("Bad Faith")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 (critique of Freudian meta-psychology, the "homuncular fallacy" of the censor); IEP §4b.

**Status:** draft

---

### Sincerity Can Also Be Bad Faith

**Claim:** Sartre argues that trying to be perfectly "sincere" — to simply be, and honestly report, what you really are — is itself impossible and can become another form of bad faith.

**Explanation:** It sounds like the cure for bad faith would just be honesty: stop performing, stop hiding, just admit what you really are. Sartre says this doesn't work, because there's no fixed "what you really are" sitting there waiting to be honestly reported — you're always in the middle of becoming something, never a finished fact you could just describe accurately. So a demand for total sincerity ("just admit you're a coward," "just admit you're in love") can trick you into treating yourself as a settled object again, which is the same move bad faith makes. For Sartre, this is why bad faith is so hard to escape by willpower alone — even the attempt to be honest can smuggle the old mistake back in.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 2 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.2 — "No project of 'sincerity' (strictly being what one is) remains possible given the for-itself's structure."

**Status:** draft

## D. Being-For-Itself vs. Being-In-Itself

### Being-in-Itself: Things Just Are What They Are

**Claim:** Sartre calls the mode of being of ordinary objects "being-in-itself": complete, self-identical, and lacking any inner distance from themselves.

**Explanation:** A rock, a table, a tree — Sartre says these just *are* what they are, completely and without any gap. A rock isn't secretly hoping to become something else, or troubled by what it used to be, or aware of itself at all; it's simply, fully, itself, the way "5" is simply the number 5. Sartre uses this as a contrast case: it's exactly what conscious beings are *not* like, and setting it up carefully is how he gets leverage to explain what's distinctive about being a person.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Introduction (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (being-in-itself as "pure plenitude"); IEP §3b (Two Types of Being).

**Status:** draft

---

### Being-for-Itself: Consciousness Is What It Is Not

**Claim:** Sartre argues that consciousness (which he calls "being-for-itself") never simply coincides with itself the way an object does — it's always ahead of, or beyond, whatever it currently is.

**Explanation:** Here's the strange-sounding but central Sartrean idea: you are never just "you" the way a rock is just a rock. Whatever you are right now — anxious, a student, in love — you're also, at the very same moment, capable of stepping back from it, questioning it, and heading toward something else. Sartre sums this up in a deliberately paradoxical phrase: consciousness "is what it is not, and is not what it is." It sounds like a contradiction, but the plain idea behind it is simple — you're always partly defined by a past that's no longer quite you, and always reaching toward a future that isn't yet you either. That restlessness, not any fixed inner core, is what a self actually is.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Two, Ch. 1 ("Immediate Structures of the For-itself")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (the for-itself as "a being which is what it is not and is not what it is"); IEP §4a (A Lack of Self-Identity).

**Status:** draft

### Humans as "Nothingness" at the Core of Being

**Claim:** Sartre argues that what makes consciousness different from objects is that it introduces "nothingness" — the capacity to negate, question, and imagine what is not the case — into the world.

**Explanation:** "Nothingness" sounds bleak, almost like Sartre is saying people are worthless or empty in a value sense — he isn't. He means something more technical and, actually, empowering: only a conscious being can notice an absence (like a friend who *isn't* at the café you expected them at), imagine something that doesn't exist yet, or ask "what if things were otherwise?" A rock can't do any of that — it has no relationship to what it lacks or what it isn't. Sartre's claim is that this power to relate to "what is not" is exactly what freedom is made of, and it's unique to conscious beings. Being a "nothing" at your core, in this sense, is what makes you capable of becoming anything at all.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 ("The Origin of Negation")

**Misreadings (optional):** "Nothingness" here does not mean nihilism or meaninglessness — it's Sartre's technical term for consciousness's capacity to negate and imagine, which is the very engine of freedom, not a claim that nothing matters.

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 ("consciousness is a being by whom nothingness comes into the world"); IEP §3c (Nothingness).

**Status:** draft

### Conscious Experience Is Prereflectively Aware of Itself

**Claim:** Sartre holds that every conscious experience includes a non-reflective awareness of itself and requires no hidden inner ego behind it; this does not make reflective self-interpretation infallible.

**Explanation:** We often talk as if a “real me” sits behind experience like a little person at a control panel. Sartre rejects that picture: consciousness is directed toward an object and is, at the same time, non-positionally aware of itself. But “transparent” must be handled carefully. Sartre argues that reflection can overreach—for example, moving from a present feeling of repulsion to the enduring claim “I hate him”—and his account of bad faith depends on our ability to misdescribe ourselves. What he denies is a hidden ego or mental container required to make an experience conscious, not every possibility of self-error.

**Citations:**
- *The Transcendence of the Ego*, especially the discussion of reflection and the “I hate” judgment
- *Being and Nothingness*, Introduction, “The Pre-Reflective Cogito and the Being of the *Percipere*”

**Provenance:** SEP §2, on the transparency of intentional experience and the strict limits of reflection; SEP §4.2 on the critique of the Freudian unconscious; IEP §3b/§4b

**Status:** draft

---

### We Never Coincide With Ourselves

**Claim:** Sartre argues that the structure of time means a person is never simply identical to their past self or their future self.

**Explanation:** Think about saying "I used to be shy." The moment you say it, you've already put some distance between "shy me" and "me right now" — you're describing a past self you're no longer simply identical with. The same goes the other direction: whatever you're going to become tomorrow isn't fixed yet, so you're not identical with your future self either. Sartre thinks this three-way non-coincidence (with your past, and with your future) is basic to what it is to be a person moving through time — you're always somewhere between who you were and who you're going to be, never simply pinned down as one settled thing.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Two, Ch. 2 ("Temporality")

**Provenance:** IEP §4a — "the for-itself never coincides with itself: it 'is not identical with its past nor its future.'"

**Status:** draft

### Nothingness Enters the World Through Us

**Claim:** Sartre's example of expecting to meet a friend named Pierre at a café and finding him absent shows how negation and absence are real features of lived experience, not just abstract logical facts.

**Explanation:** Imagine walking into a café expecting to find your friend Pierre there, and he's not. You don't just coldly register the fact "Pierre is not spatially located here" — you *feel* his absence pervading the whole café; the place seems haunted by a specific missing person, not just generally empty. Sartre uses this to argue that absence and negation aren't just things we tack onto the world afterward in our heads — they show up as part of the world we actually experience, but only because a conscious being (who was expecting something specific) is there to experience it that way. No consciousness, no experienced absence — just stuff sitting around, indifferent to what isn't there.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 ("The Origin of Negation")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (the Pierre-at-the-café example); IEP §3c (Nothingness).

**Status:** draft

## E. The Look and Being-for-Others

### The Look: Becoming an Object in Someone Else's World

**Claim:** Sartre argues that when another person perceives you, you experience yourself as an object seen from outside — what he calls "the Look" — and this is a distinct kind of experience, not an inference.

**Explanation:** Imagine you're peeping through a keyhole, completely absorbed in what you're watching, not even thinking about yourself. Then you hear footsteps behind you. Instantly, everything changes: you're no longer just an absorbed watcher, you're suddenly aware of yourself as someone who could be seen — caught, judged, described — by another person. Sartre calls this shift "the Look." It's not that you reasoned your way to "someone is probably watching me and forming opinions" — the sense of being seen hits you immediately, almost bodily, before any thinking happens. This experience, Sartre argues, is how we really encounter other minds — not through argument, but through the lived, often uncomfortable, sense of being turned into an object for somebody else.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 1 ("The Existence of Others")

**Provenance:** SEP §4.3 (the keyhole scene); IEP §5a (The Problem of Other Minds).

**Status:** draft

---

### Shame as Evidence of Other Minds

**Claim:** Sartre argues that the experience of shame at being seen doing something is what actually grounds our certainty that other minds exist — not philosophical inference.

**Explanation:** Philosophers have long worried about the "problem of other minds" — how do you really know other people have inner lives at all, rather than just behaving like conscious beings? Sartre thinks he has a shortcut past the whole puzzle: shame. In the keyhole scenario, the shame you feel at being caught only makes sense if there really is another consciousness there, judging you from its own point of view — shame isn't a feeling you could have in a world of mere objects. So for Sartre, certain experiences (shame chief among them) don't just suggest other minds exist, they require it; you couldn't have the experience at all in a solipsistic world where you were the only conscious being.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 1 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.3 ("pre-moral shame... a shudder recognizing that one is the object the other sees"); IEP §5a.

**Status:** draft

### In *Being and Nothingness*, Conflict Structures Relations with Others

**Claim:** In *Being and Nothingness*, Sartre argues that conflict is the basic structure of relations between consciousnesses; his later *Critique of Dialectical Reason* also analyzes reciprocal collective action.

**Explanation:** This is one of the early Sartre's bleaker claims. In *Being and Nothingness*, each person experiences the other's gaze as objectifying and tries to recover their own position as subject, so Sartre calls conflict—not harmonious “being-with”—the essence of relations between consciousnesses. But this is not his entire career's final social theory. In the later *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, he analyzes a “group-in-fusion” whose members can recognize one another reciprocally while acting toward a shared end, even though he thinks such groups remain unstable and risk hardening into institutions.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 3 ("Concrete Relations with Others")
- *Critique of Dialectical Reason*, Book I, Part II, on the group-in-fusion

**Provenance:** SEP §4.3 on conflict in *Being and Nothingness* and §6.3 on reciprocity in the group-in-fusion; IEP §5b (Human Relationships)

**Status:** draft

### Sadism and Masochism as Two Failed Escapes from the Look

**Claim:** Sartre analyzes sadism and masochism as two opposite strategies for dealing with the conflict of the Look, both of which he thinks ultimately fail.

**Explanation:** If being seen by someone turns you into an object, and seeing them does the same to them, Sartre says you have two basic strategies. One is to try to dominate — treat the other person as pure object, deny that they have their own point of view, and refuse to be affected by their Look (what he calls, using the technical psychological terms of his day, a "sadistic" strategy). The other is to give in completely — let yourself be nothing but what the other person sees you as, surrendering your own perspective to theirs (a "masochistic" strategy). Sartre argues both fail, because neither actually resolves the underlying standoff between two free consciousnesses — you can't make someone stop being a subject just by treating them like an object, and giving up your own subjectivity doesn't actually make it disappear either.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 3 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.3 ("This appears as sadism... or masochism... Both strategies represent futile attempts to escape fundamental otherness.").

**Status:** draft

---

### Love as a Contradictory Project

**Claim:** Sartre argues that romantic love, as usually understood, involves an impossible demand: wanting to possess another person while also wanting them to remain fully free.

**Explanation:** What does a lover actually want? Sartre argues it's not just physical closeness or company — the lover wants to be loved *freely chosen*, specifically and irreplaceably, by someone whose love still counts as a free gift rather than something coerced or automatic. But that's a bind: if you could somehow guarantee or control the other person's love, it would stop counting as a free choice and become worthless to you; yet if you leave it entirely up to their freedom, you can never actually secure it. Sartre thinks this tension is built into the very structure of wanting to be loved, which is part of why he treats love, on his account, as inherently unstable rather than as a stable resting point.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 3 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** IEP §5b (Human Relationships — love's structure mirroring the desire for being); SEP §4.3 (sadism/masochism/love as forms of conflict).

**Status:** draft

### Why "We" Experiences Don't Refute the Conflict Thesis

**Claim:** Sartre acknowledges shared "we" experiences (like an audience watching a concert together) but argues these are psychologically real without undermining the deeper claim that conflict is the basic structure between consciousnesses.

**Explanation:** Sartre isn't blind to the fact that people often feel a genuine sense of togetherness — a crowd at a concert, a team working as one. He calls this a "we-subject" experience and admits it's real. But he argues it's a surface-level, changeable mood rather than something that overturns his deeper claim about how consciousnesses are fundamentally related to each other. On his view, this shared feeling depends on everyone jointly looking at some third thing (the stage, the game) rather than looking at *each other* — the moment attention turns back onto one another directly, the more basic subject/object tension he describes reappears. So "we" moments are compatible with his theory; they're just not, for him, evidence against it.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part Three, Ch. 1 (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.3 ("Sartre distinguishes co-spectatorship... from genuine ontological grounding of other knowledge. Such shared subjectivity is merely 'subjective and psychological'").

**Status:** draft

## F. Anguish

### Anguish as the Recognition of Freedom

**Claim:** Sartre argues that "anguish" is the specific feeling of realizing that you, and only you, are responsible for your choices, with nothing outside you to fall back on.

**Explanation:** Picture standing at the edge of a cliff. Fear is being afraid of falling. Anguish, in Sartre's technical sense, is something different and stranger: a dizzying awareness that nothing is actually stopping *you* from jumping — no invisible force field of "who I am" guarantees you'll step back. The danger isn't just what's out there; it's that your own next move is entirely undetermined and up to you. Sartre thinks this same vertigo shows up whenever we're honest with ourselves about big life choices — there's no rulebook and no fixed "self" guaranteeing what we'll do, only our own freedom, staring back at us.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 ("The Origin of Negation")
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)

**Provenance:** SEP §4.1 (freedom as constitutive, negation and freedom linked); general knowledge of the "cliff-edge vertigo" illustration used across secondary literature on Sartrean anguish; concept confirmed present in both Being and Nothingness's treatment of freedom and the 1945 lecture's discussion of anguish.

**Status:** draft

### Anguish Is Not the Same as Fear

**Claim:** Sartre distinguishes anguish (anxiety about your own freedom) from ordinary fear (being afraid of some external threat).

**Explanation:** Fear has an object outside you — a growling dog, a falling rock, a threatening person. Anguish, in Sartre's sense, is different: it's not about a threat in the world, it's about *you*, and specifically about the fact that no fixed self or rule determines what you'll do next. You can feel afraid of losing your job; you feel anguish when you realize that even your commitment to keeping it — your resolutions, your values, your sense of who you are — doesn't automatically guarantee you'll act accordingly, because you have to keep choosing to act on it, moment by moment, with nothing forcing your hand.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Part One, Ch. 1 (location approximate)

**Misreadings (optional):** "Anguish" is often confused with generalized anxiety or worry in the everyday sense; Sartre's technical use points specifically at the vertiginous recognition of your own freedom, not fear of an external danger.

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the classic fear/anguish distinction Sartre draws (well documented across secondary literature on Being and Nothingness Part One), consistent with SEP §4.1's account of freedom as an ontological structure rather than a worldly threat.

**Status:** draft

---

### Abandonment: No God, No Guarantees

**Claim:** Sartre's notion of "abandonment" (or forlornness) is the recognition that, without God, there is no external source that can hand down values or guarantee what we ought to do.

**Explanation:** If there were a God who designed human beings for a purpose, that purpose could, in principle, tell you what's genuinely worthwhile — like reading the manufacturer's instructions for a machine. Sartre argues that without any such author of human nature, that safety net disappears: nothing out there guarantees in advance that any value is objectively binding on you. He calls this situation "abandonment" — we've been left, so to speak, without a parent figure or a rulebook, and have to generate and stand behind our own values, fully aware that no outside authority backs them up.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate — this is one of the lecture's three named concepts, alongside anguish and despair)

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the lecture's structure (it explicitly organizes its argument around anguish, abandonment, and despair); consistent with SEP's characterization of the lecture as a popularization of the ontology in Being and Nothingness (SEP §1).

**Status:** draft

### Despair: Acting Without Certainty of Success

**Claim:** Sartre's notion of "despair" means acting on the basis of what is actually within your power, without counting on favorable outcomes you can't control.

**Explanation:** This isn't despair in the everyday sense of hopeless gloom. Sartre means something more like clear-eyed realism: when you act, you should only really rely on the parts of the outcome that depend on your own choices and effort — not on how other people will respond, or how the world will cooperate, since none of that is within your control. If you're planning a project that depends on a friend keeping a promise, "despair" in Sartre's sense means acting without banking on their reliability as a sure thing, because you can't actually guarantee anyone else's freedom, only your own commitment. It's a call to stay grounded in what you can actually answer for.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate — the third of the lecture's three named concepts, alongside anguish and abandonment)

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the lecture's structure, consistent with SEP §4.1's emphasis on freedom and responsibility as always located in what the agent themselves does.

**Status:** draft

## G. Humanism and Its Relation to the Technical Ontology

### "Existentialism Is a Humanism": A Popular Lecture, Not the Full System

**Claim:** Sartre's 1945 lecture "Existentialism is a Humanism" is a simplified, public-facing defense of existentialism, not a substitute for the technical arguments of Being and Nothingness.

**Explanation:** In 1945, Sartre gave a hugely popular public lecture (thousands of Parisians reportedly attended) responding to critics — religious thinkers who thought existentialism led to despair, and Marxists who thought it was self-indulgent individualism. To reach a general audience, the lecture uses much plainer language and simpler examples than his dense 1943 treatise Being and Nothingness, which works out the same ideas through detailed, technical phenomenology. That accessibility is exactly why the lecture became so famous — and also why it can be misleading if treated as the complete picture: it's a sketch and a defense, aimed at a specific controversy, not the full architecture behind it.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism
- Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Misreadings (optional):** Treating the lecture as if it fully captures or replaces the detailed ontology of Being and Nothingness — Sartre wrote it to popularize and defend existentialism against specific critics, and it simplifies accordingly.

**Provenance:** SEP §1 (Life and Works) — the lecture as "an accessible version of his difficult treatise, Being and Nothingness," responding to "Marxist and Christian critics."

**Status:** draft

---

### Sartre Later Distanced Himself from Parts of the Humanism Lecture

**Claim:** Sartre later expressed reservations about aspects of "Existentialism is a Humanism," regarding it as a hastily simplified account he did not fully stand behind in the same form.

**Explanation:** The lecture made Sartre a household name, but its popularizing style — clear, punchy, aimed at a live debate — came at the cost of some of the nuance in his more careful philosophical work. In later interviews and writings, Sartre is generally understood to have treated the lecture with some ambivalence, as something written quickly for a specific rhetorical occasion rather than as his considered last word on ethics and freedom. This matters for how the lecture should be used as a source: it's a genuine and useful entry point into his thought, but not one he treated as final or beyond revision.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate — Sartre's later distancing is reported in secondary/biographical literature rather than in the lecture text itself)

**Misreadings (optional):** Assuming the 1945 lecture represents Sartre's settled, permanent view — his own later attitude toward it was more qualified than its enduring popularity might suggest.

**Provenance:** General biographical knowledge of Sartre's later ambivalence about the lecture; the SEP entry itself does not discuss this distancing explicitly (confirmed by direct query — SEP §1 presents the lecture and Being and Nothingness as complementary without noting later reservations), so this card leans on broader secondary literature and should be treated as a well-attested but not SEP/IEP-sourced claim.

**Status:** draft

### Existentialism Is an Optimistic Doctrine of Action, Not Nihilism

**Claim:** Sartre explicitly rejects the charge that existentialism is a nihilistic or despairing philosophy, presenting it instead as a doctrine centered on action and self-making.

**Explanation:** A common knock against existentialism, which Sartre was directly responding to in his 1945 lecture, is that if there's no God and no fixed values, then nothing matters and anything goes. Sartre pushes back hard on this. His view isn't that nothing matters — it's that *what* matters is something we have to actively create and commit to through our own choices and actions, rather than discover ready-made. Far from license for passivity or despair, he presents this as a demanding, even strenuous, call to responsibility: since there's no one else to do it for you, what you make of your life is entirely up to your own action.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)

**Misreadings (optional):** The common misreading that "existentialism means nihilism" or "nothing matters" gets it backwards — Sartre presents existentialism as demanding that we take responsibility for creating value through action, precisely because nothing else will do it for us.

**Provenance:** General knowledge of the lecture's explicit rebuttal of the nihilism charge; consistent with SEP §4.1's account of freedom as requiring the agent to "posit" the significance of their situation through choice.

**Status:** draft

---

### Authenticity Has an Ethical Dimension, Even Without Fixed Values

**Claim:** Sartre holds that "authenticity" — properly owning both your freedom and your factual situation — carries genuine ethical weight, even though he denies any pre-given moral rules.

**Explanation:** It might seem like a philosophy with no fixed values couldn't tell you to act any particular way. But Sartre argues there's still a meaningful difference between authentic and inauthentic ways of living: authenticity means owning up to the fact that you're both shaped by your circumstances (facticity) and always free to respond to them in new ways (transcendence), instead of collapsing into one side or the other through bad faith. This isn't the same as saying "just do whatever you spontaneously feel like" — Sartre's authentic agent takes responsibility precisely by recognizing how their freedom and their situation fit together, and by owning the values their choices create.

**Citations:**
- Existentialism is a Humanism (location approximate)
- Being and Nothingness (location approximate — the promised "ethics" is left largely for a later, unfinished work)

**Misreadings (optional):** Authenticity is sometimes read as simply "follow your spontaneous impulses" — Sartre's actual criterion is the proper coordination of freedom and factual situation, not the unchecked priority of spontaneity.

**Provenance:** IEP §6b–6c (Authenticity; An Ethical Dimension) — "a recognition of how our freedom interacts with our facticity exhibits the responsibility which we have to make proper choices."

**Status:** draft

## H. The Marxist Turn: Critique of Dialectical Reason

### The Turn Toward Marxism

**Claim:** In his later work, especially Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960), Sartre tried to combine his existentialist account of individual freedom with Marxist analysis of history and social class.

**Explanation:** Sartre's wartime and postwar political experience pushed him to take seriously the Marxist claim that history is shaped by large-scale material and economic forces, not just individual choices. But he didn't want to give up his earlier insistence that individuals are never simply products of their class or economic position. His later big project tries to hold both together: yes, we're born into social and economic structures we didn't choose, and yes, those structures really constrain and shape what's possible for us — but individuals still take up those structures through their own free activity, which Sartre calls "praxis," rather than being fully determined by them.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 (Existentialist Marxism); IEP §7a (Critique of Dialectical Reason).

**Status:** draft

### Scarcity as the Material Root of Social Conflict

**Claim:** In the Critique, Sartre argues that material scarcity — not just human nature or psychology — is a basic driver of conflict and alienation in social life.

**Explanation:** Sartre's earlier work locates conflict between people in the very structure of consciousness itself (see: the Look). In his later Marxist-influenced work, he adds an important material dimension: much real-world conflict exists because there isn't enough to go around. When resources are scarce, my getting what I need can genuinely come at your expense, turning other people into potential threats or competitors in a very concrete, economic way, on top of whatever conflict already exists at the level of consciousness. This lets Sartre connect his abstract philosophical account of conflict to actual economic and historical conditions — poverty, competition for jobs, land, or resources — rather than treating conflict as a purely psychological or metaphysical fact.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 (practico-inert, scarcity as background condition of seriality); general Marxist-influenced reading Sartre develops in the Critique.

**Status:** draft

### Seriality: Isolated Individuals United Only by Function

**Claim:** Sartre uses the term "seriality" to describe a common social condition where people are grouped together only by an external role or function, remaining isolated and interchangeable with one another.

**Explanation:** Think of people standing in line for a bus. They're all doing the "same thing" and are, in a sense, united — but only in a thin, external way: each person is interchangeable with any other person in the line, they're not working together or recognizing each other as individuals, and they might even be secretly competing for the last seat. Sartre calls this kind of grouping "seriality," and argues it's actually the default condition of a lot of modern social life — consumers, commuters, radio listeners — where people are lumped together by circumstance or function while remaining fundamentally separate, anonymous, and often subtly pitted against each other.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 ("Seriality: The dominant social form where individuals are substitutable, anonymous, and separated despite apparent unity. Waiting for transport... exemplifies serial existence").

### The Group-in-Fusion: Freedom Born from Collective Crisis

**Claim:** Sartre argues that in moments of shared crisis, isolated individuals ("seriality") can suddenly unite into a genuinely cooperative group he calls a "group-in-fusion."

**Explanation:** Picture a crowd of separate, anonymous strangers suddenly acting as one — say, during a spontaneous uprising or a shared emergency — where instead of just standing in the same place doing the same isolated thing, people start actually coordinating, recognizing each other, and acting together toward a common goal. Sartre calls this dramatic shift the birth of a "group-in-fusion," and treats it as a real, if temporary, experience of collective freedom that breaks through the isolation of ordinary social life. It's one of the more hopeful notes in his later work — evidence that solidarity, not just conflict, is a real possibility between people, even if (as later cards discuss) it doesn't tend to last.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 ("Group-in-Fusion: A rupture occurring when serial individuals spontaneously unite through shared praxis toward common goals... demonstrates authentic reciprocity and freedom").

**Status:** draft

### The Practico-Inert: How Past Human Action Constrains Future Freedom

**Claim:** Sartre's concept of the "practico-inert" describes how the accumulated results of past human activity harden into material conditions that constrain the freedom of people acting later.

**Explanation:** Roads, factories, laws, economic systems — all of these were originally built by human activity and choices, but once in place, they take on a life of their own, shaping and limiting what later generations can do, almost like a second nature made of frozen human history. Sartre calls this the "practico-inert" — literally, the practical (human-made) turned inert (thing-like). It's a way of taking seriously how real, material structures inherited from the past genuinely constrain freedom, without giving up his basic claim that people remain free — they're free within and against conditions that were themselves made, at some point, by other free people's actions.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 (Practico-Inert — "the activity of others insofar as it is sustained and diverted by inorganic inertia").

**Status:** draft

### Institutions Reintroduce the Very Alienation Revolutions Try to Escape

**Claim:** Sartre argues that groups formed through spontaneous collective action (group-in-fusion) tend to re-solidify into institutions, which reintroduce hierarchy and the isolation the group was trying to escape.

**Explanation:** The exciting collective unity of a group-in-fusion — think of the early days of a revolutionary movement or a spontaneous protest — doesn't stay that way forever. To survive and keep acting over time, the group has to organize: assign roles, create leaders, set up rules for who decides what. Sartre argues this process, while practically necessary, tends to recreate exactly the kind of hierarchy, distance, and anonymous role-playing (seriality) the group originally formed to escape. This is one of the more pessimistic threads in his later work — a kind of built-in tendency for liberating collective moments to calcify back into the alienating structures they rose up against.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 ("The Ontological Check: Groups inevitably formalize into institutions, reintroducing hierarchies... and relapsing into seriality").

**Status:** draft

### Dialectical Reason vs. Analytical Reason

**Claim:** Sartre argues that understanding history and social life properly requires "dialectical reason" — thinking that tracks how wholes and processes develop over time — rather than "analytical reason," which breaks things into separate, static pieces.

**Explanation:** Analytical reason is the kind of thinking that takes a complicated situation and breaks it down into separate, fixed variables you can examine one at a time — useful for a lot of purposes, but Sartre thinks it misses something crucial about history and society. Social life, he argues, is made of ongoing human activity (what he calls "praxis") constantly acting on and being shaped by a changing whole — individuals shaping groups, groups shaping individuals, structures shaping activity that then reshapes the structures. "Dialectical reason" is Sartre's name for the kind of thinking that can track this back-and-forth, evolving totality, rather than freezing it into separate static parts the way analytical reason does.

**Citations:**
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 ("Dialectical vs. Analytical Reason: Sartre privileges dialectical reason—capturing totalization and praxis—over analytical approaches that fragment phenomena into static categories").

**Status:** draft

### The Critique Revises, Rather Than Abandons, Being and Nothingness

**Claim:** Sartre's later Marxist work does not reject his earlier existentialism but revises it — adding collective, material, and historical dimensions to a view that had earlier centered on individual consciousness.

**Explanation:** It's tempting to think Sartre simply swapped one philosophy for another — existentialism in the 1940s, Marxism in the 1960s. That's too simple. His later work keeps the core commitment that individuals are never fully reducible to, or determined by, their circumstances (whether those circumstances are psychological, as in his early work, or economic and historical, as Marxism emphasizes). What changes is the scale and richness of the constraints he takes seriously: Being and Nothingness mostly analyzes freedom and conflict between individual consciousnesses, while the Critique adds groups, institutions, scarcity, and historical material conditions into the picture. Freedom remains central throughout — it's just increasingly understood as freedom operating inside thick, shared, historically inherited conditions rather than in a more abstract, individual-focused setting.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness (1943)
- Critique of Dialectical Reason, Vol. 1 (1960) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §6 (closing characterization: the Critique "revises Being and Nothingness's emphasis on dyadic consciousness and individual freedom, introducing collective mediations and material conditions while maintaining freedom's irreducibility"); IEP §7a-b.

**Status:** draft

## I. Background, Method, and Applications

### Intentionality: Consciousness Is Always Consciousness "Of" Something

**Claim:** Following Husserl, Sartre holds that consciousness is never just a free-floating inner state — it's always directed at, or "of," something outside itself.

**Explanation:** This is a foundational idea Sartre took from the philosopher Edmund Husserl and then built his whole approach on: there's no such thing as just "being conscious" in the abstract, the way you might picture a glowing inner light. Consciousness is always consciousness *of* something — of a tree, a memory, a fear, an idea. Sartre pushed this further than Husserl by arguing it means consciousness has no private inner content of its own at all; it's entirely a relationship pointed outward at the world, which is part of why he later denies there's any hidden inner self behind it (see the card on consciousness having no hidden interior).

**Citations:**
- The Transcendence of the Ego (1936) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §2 (Transcendence of the Ego: The Discovery of Intentionality) — "intentionality allows us to discard the metaphysical oppositions between the inner and outer."

**Status:** draft

### The Self Is Something We Construct, Not a Hidden Inner Ego

**Claim:** In his early work The Transcendence of the Ego, Sartre argues that the "self" or "ego" is not a hidden inner subject behind all experience, but something consciousness itself produces when it reflects on its own past activity.

**Explanation:** Many philosophers (including Sartre's teacher Husserl, following Kant) assumed there must be a stable "I" tucked behind every experience, silently accompanying all your thoughts. Sartre disagreed. He noticed that when you're fully absorbed in something — chasing a bus, getting lost in a book — there's no "I" showing up in your experience at all; it's just the chasing, the reading. Only when you stop and reflect ("I was so absorbed just now") does an "ego" appear — and even then, Sartre argues, it's something consciousness is constructing about itself after the fact, not a pre-existing inner owner of experience found by looking inward. This is an early, important piece of groundwork for his later claim that there's no fixed self or human nature to fall back on.

**Citations:**
- The Transcendence of the Ego (1936) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §2 (the running-after-a-streetcar and portrait-contemplation examples, "no I"); IEP §2b (The Ego).

**Status:** draft

### Existential Psychoanalysis: Interpreting a Person's Fundamental Choice

**Claim:** Sartre proposes replacing Freudian psychoanalysis with "existential psychoanalysis," which interprets all of a person's behavior as expressing one deep, freely chosen orientation toward life rather than unconscious drives.

**Explanation:** Instead of Freud's unconscious drives and childhood traumas working behind the scenes, Sartre suggests that a person's whole personality — their tastes, habits, even small quirks — can be read as different expressions of one deep, original choice about how to exist, which he calls the "fundamental project." He uses the poet Baudelaire as an example: seemingly unrelated facts about him (his taste in food, his attitudes toward nature) all turn out, on Sartre's reading, to reflect one underlying stance toward existence. Crucially, Sartre insists this fundamental choice is something the person actually lives and experiences — just not something they explicitly *know* about themselves in so many words, which is why it takes an interpretive, almost detective-like method to uncover it, rather than digging into a hidden unconscious.

**Citations:**
- Being and Nothingness, Conclusion (location approximate)
- Baudelaire (1946) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §5 (Existential Psychoanalysis and the Fundamental Project); IEP §4c.

**Status:** draft

### Literature as an Appeal to the Reader's Freedom

**Claim:** Sartre argues that literature, especially the novel, works by appealing to and requiring the reader's own freedom to complete the meaning of the work.

**Explanation:** For Sartre, a novel isn't just a container of facts to absorb passively — it's something more like an invitation. The writer sets a fictional world in motion, but it's the reader's own imaginative freedom that actually brings the characters and events to life while reading, filling in and responding to what's on the page. Because of this, Sartre thought serious literature is bound up with freedom on both ends: it's produced by an author disclosing the world as shaped by human choice, and it only becomes real experience through a reader actively exercising their own freedom to engage with it — which is part of why he thought writing was never a politically neutral act.

**Citations:**
- What Is Literature? (1948) — location approximate

**Provenance:** SEP §3 (Imagination, Phenomenology and Literature) — literature's "one true subject" as freedom; the reader as sharing "responsibility for the fictional world's meaning."

### Racism and Colonial Domination as Institutionalized Bad Faith

**Claim:** Sartre analyzed racism and colonial oppression as forms of bad faith at a social scale — treating people as fixed essences (a "type") rather than as free individuals.

**Explanation:** Bad faith isn't just a private, individual habit for Sartre — he also applied the idea to large-scale social evils. Racism, on his analysis, works by treating a whole group of people as if they had a fixed, essential nature (lazy, dangerous, inferior — pick the stereotype) rather than as individuals who, like anyone, exist first and only become anything through their own choices. This is exactly the kind of essentialist thinking his whole philosophy is built to reject. Sartre extended this to colonialism and antisemitism as well, arguing that entire societies, not just individual bigots, sustain these systems through willful ignorance and unearned comfort — making collective bad faith, on his view, a real driver of historical injustice.

## Metaphysics

### Act and Potency

**Claim:**

Change is possible because things have both an actual, realized nature and unrealized capacities that can become real.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas borrows this idea from Aristotle to solve a puzzle: how can something become different without simply popping into and out of existence? His answer is that everyday things are a mix of two aspects. "Actuality" is whatever a thing currently, really is — what it fully, actually is right now. "Potentiality" is a real, built-in capacity to become something it isn't yet — an acorn isn't an oak, but it has a genuine, specific capacity to become one, unlike a rock, which doesn't. Change is just a capacity getting realized. Aquinas ties this to matter (the "raw capacity" side of a physical thing) and form (the "this is what it actually is" side).

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Principiis Naturae, ch. 1
- Summa Theologiae (ST) I, Q.9, a.1 (only God is pure actuality with no unrealized potential)

**Misreadings (optional):**

"Potency" here doesn't mean anything like physics' "potential energy" — it's a broader idea about built-in, thing-specific capacity to become something, not a quantity of stored energy.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter ("Potentiality and Actuality"); IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics ("Act and Potency, Essence and Existence")

**Status:** draft

### Substantial Change vs. Accidental Change

**Claim:**

Aquinas distinguishes surface-level changes a thing survives from deep changes in which the thing itself stops existing.

**Explanation:**

When a leaf changes color, the tree is still the same tree — that's an "accidental" change, a change in some feature the thing has, not in what the thing fundamentally is. But when the tree dies and rots away, the tree itself stops existing — that's a "substantial" change, since the underlying thing itself, not just a feature of it, is gone. Aquinas says accidental changes involve gaining or losing a property while the same substance persists, while substantial changes involve one kind of thing actually ceasing and (often) a different kind of thing beginning in its place.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Principiis Naturae, ch. 1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter (overview of hylomorphism)

**Status:** draft

### Prime Matter as Pure Potential

**Claim:**

Underlying every substantial change is "prime matter": a substratum with no features of its own, pure raw capacity to become something.

**Explanation:**

When a tree dies and decays, something has to persist through the change for it to count as one thing turning into another rather than one thing vanishing and an unrelated thing appearing from nowhere. Aquinas calls that underlying "something" prime matter — and he means it has literally no properties of its own; it's pure potential, not an actual stuff or ingredient you could point to. This creates a real philosophical puzzle: if prime matter has zero actual features, in what sense does it exist at all? Aquinas's own answer is debated by scholars, and many later medieval thinkers found the idea of something with zero actuality too strange to accept.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Principiis Naturae, ch. 2

**Misreadings (optional):**

Prime matter is not simply "physical stuff" or "atoms" in the modern sense — it's a metaphysical placeholder for pure potentiality, not an observable material.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter ("Prime Matter and Conceptual Difficulties")

**Status:** draft

### One Substantial Form Per Thing

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that a living thing has exactly one deep, unifying "what-it-is" — not several stacked "souls" or forms layered together.

**Explanation:**

Medieval thinkers debated whether a human being might have separate layers making them alive, sensing, and thinking, stacked like building blocks. Aquinas rejects this "layered" picture. He argues that a genuine single thing needs a single unifying principle running through every part of it — otherwise you don't have one thing, you have a pile of parts sitting next to each other, like a heap of sand. So for Aquinas, one soul does all the jobs (life, sensation, thought) in a human being, not three different souls stacked up. This single-form view was controversial even in his own time.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.76, a.3–4

**Misreadings (optional):**

This is a technical debate about metaphysical unity, not a claim that a human personality is "simple" — Aquinas thinks the one soul still grounds many distinct powers and activities.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter ("Unity and Individuation"); §5 Soul and Body ("The Problem of Substantial Unity")

**Status:** draft

### Matter as the Principle of Individuation

**Claim:**

Two things can share the exact same nature or "kind" and still be distinct individuals only because each is tied to a different chunk of matter.

**Explanation:**

Two humans share the same essence — being human — so what makes them two separate people rather than one? Aquinas's answer is matter: it's each person's own particular body/matter, existing here rather than there, that makes an otherwise-identical nature into two distinct individuals. Once a nature ("form," in Aquinas's vocabulary — meaning the defining pattern that makes a thing the kind of thing it is) gets tied to a specific chunk of matter, it becomes this individual rather than a general type, and holds onto that individual identity afterward.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Ente et Essentia, ch. 2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter ("Unity and Individuation"); IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics

**Status:** draft

### Essence and Existence Are Really Distinct in Creatures

**Claim:**

In everything except God, what a thing is (its essence) is really different from the fact that it exists at all.

**Explanation:**

You can fully understand what a unicorn would be — its essence, its definition — without that telling you whether any unicorns actually exist. Aquinas thinks this gap between "what something is" and "whether it exists" is real in every created thing, not just a trick of language. Even for things that do exist, like you or a rock, their existing is not part of their basic definition — it had to be added, from outside, by a cause. This is why created things are dependent and contingent: nothing about being human, in itself, guarantees that any humans exist. God is the one exception, discussed separately.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Ente et Essentia, ch. 4
- ST I, Q.3, a.4 (applied by contrast to God)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("Essence and Existence in God"); IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics ("Being qua Being: Act and Potency, Essence and Existence")

**Status:** draft

### The Four Causes

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that fully explaining any natural thing requires identifying four distinct kinds of explanation, not just one.

**Explanation:**

Following Aristotle, Aquinas says a complete explanation of anything answers four different questions: what it's made of (material cause), what made or produces it (efficient cause), what pattern or structure makes it the kind of thing it is (formal cause), and what it's for or tends toward (final cause). Take a chair: its wood is the material cause, the carpenter is the efficient cause, the design or shape is the formal cause, and "something to sit on" is the final cause. Modern science mostly asks only about material and efficient causes; Aquinas thinks that's an incomplete picture of explanation.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Principiis Naturae, ch. 3–4

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology ("The Extension of Science and The Four Causes")

**Status:** draft

---

### Final Causality Is Foundational

**Claim:**

For Aquinas, a thing's tendency toward some goal or outcome is what makes its other kinds of causes make sense at all.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas thinks purpose or directedness — what he calls "final cause" — isn't just one item on a list alongside the others; it's what ties the others together. A thing acts as a cause of anything only because it's the kind of thing that's aimed at producing certain effects. Even things with no mind, like plants growing toward sunlight or acorns reliably becoming oaks rather than something else, count as having a "final cause" in this sense — a built-in tendency toward a characteristic outcome, not a conscious goal.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Principiis Naturae, ch. 4

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology ("The Extension of Science and The Four Causes" — Final Cause)

**Status:** draft

### Being Is Said in Many Ways

**Claim:**

For Aquinas, "to exist" doesn't mean exactly the same thing when said of a substance, a property, and God — the word shifts sense each time.

**Explanation:**

Strictly speaking, Aquinas says, only whole individual things — a person, a tree, a rock — properly "exist" in the primary sense of the word. Properties and features of those things (like a tree's color or a person's height) are called "beings" too, but only in a secondary, borrowed sense: they exist only as a way that some genuine thing exists, not as things in their own right. This isn't sloppiness — Aquinas thinks "being" is inherently a flexible word that applies differently across different cases, which becomes especially important when later talking about how God "exists" compared to how creatures do.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.13, a.5 (applied specifically to language about God)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §4 Form and Matter ("Potentiality and Actuality" — on the multivocity of being); IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics ("Metaphysics as a Science")

**Status:** draft

### The Transcendentals: Being, Unity, Goodness, Truth

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that "being," "oneness," "goodness," and "truth" all pick out the very same reality, just viewed from different angles.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas thinks that whatever exists, just by existing, automatically counts as "one" (not falling apart into unrelated pieces), as "good" (worth having, to whatever degree it's complete and functioning), and as "true" (capable of being accurately understood by a mind). These aren't separate add-on properties competing with existence — they're the same underlying reality described in different respects. That's why he can say, for instance, that being and goodness are ultimately convertible: anything that exists, exists as good to some degree, and vice versa.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Veritate, Q.1, a.1

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics (proper accidents of being: "one, good, beautiful...")

**Status:** draft

## Natural Theology

### The Five Ways: Reason Alone Can Establish That God Exists

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that ordinary human reasoning about the observable world, without appeal to faith, can prove that God exists.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas offers five short arguments — the "Five Ways" — that each start from some very general, observable fact about the world (things change, things cause other things, things exist contingently, things have degrees of perfection, things behave in orderly ways) and argue that this fact could only make sense if something like God exists as its ultimate explanation. He presents these as genuine proofs, not just faith-based hunches: he thinks an honest, careful thinker, using reason alone, can be argued into accepting that a first cause of some kind must exist. What exactly that first cause is like beyond "first cause" takes further argument, which Aquinas provides elsewhere.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.3

**Misreadings (optional):**

The Five Ways are brief summaries in the Summa, meant as an introduction — Aquinas develops much fuller versions of these arguments elsewhere in his work (e.g. the Summa Contra Gentiles).

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("God's Existence: The Five Ways"); IEP: Aquinas, §6 Natural Theology

**Status:** draft

---

### The First Way: Change Requires an Ultimate Source

**Claim:**

Aquinas argues that anything changing is being changed by something else, and this chain cannot regress forever, so a first unchanged changer must exist.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas starts from the observation that things around us are constantly changing — heating up, moving, growing. His claim is that whenever something changes, it's because something else, already actual in the relevant way, is acting on it (wood doesn't heat itself up; something hot has to touch it). If you trace that chain of "something acting on something" backward, Aquinas argues it can't go back forever with nothing ultimately explaining the whole chain — there has to be a first source of change that isn't itself being changed by anything prior. He calls this "what everyone calls God," while immediately noting that much more needs to be shown about that first source before it earns the title "God" in the fuller religious sense.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.3 (the "first way")

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("God's Existence: The Five Ways")

**Status:** draft

---

### The Second Way: Things Cannot Cause Their Own Existence

**Claim:**

Aquinas argues that nothing can be the cause of itself, so a chain of causes existing right now must trace back to an uncaused first cause.

**Explanation:**

Nothing, Aquinas points out, produces its own existence — that would require it to exist before itself, which is nonsense. So everything that exists and is caused depends on something else to cause it. Aquinas argues that a chain of things-causing-other-things happening at the same time (not stretching back in the past, but operating right now, like a hand moving a stick moving a stone) can't be infinite with nothing grounding it — there has to be a first, uncaused cause currently sustaining the whole chain. This argument is about explaining why things are being caused right now, not about what happened first in time.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.3 (the "second way")

**Misreadings (optional):**

This isn't an argument about what happened at the start of time (like the Big Bang) — Aquinas is mainly concerned with a chain of simultaneous dependence, which is why he separately holds that reason cannot prove the world had a beginning in time at all.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("God's Existence: The Five Ways"); IEP: Aquinas, §6 Natural Theology ("The Five Ways and Their Common Misunderstandings")

**Status:** draft

### The Five Ways Don't Prove the Christian God Specifically

**Claim:**

Aquinas is explicit that proving a first cause or necessary being exists falls short of proving the distinctively Christian God of the Trinity.

**Explanation:**

A very common misreading treats the Five Ways as if they were meant to prove the whole Christian picture of God — three persons, incarnate in Jesus, and so on. Aquinas never claims this. He's careful to note that his arguments establish only that something like a first, unchanging, uncaused source of the universe exists — not, by themselves, that this source is all-knowing, perfectly good, or triune. He builds up the fuller picture of God's nature through further separate arguments about divine attributes, and he's explicit that some Christian claims (like the Trinity) can never be proven by reason alone at all — those require revelation, not philosophical argument.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.2, a.3
- ST I, Q.1, a.1 (on truths that exceed natural reason)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("God's Existence: The Five Ways" — noting the arguments' cautious conclusions); IEP: Aquinas, §2 Faith and Reason ("preambles" vs. "articles" of faith)

**Status:** draft

### Divine Simplicity: God Has No Parts

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that God is not made up of separable parts or components in any way — not physical parts, and not even distinct "aspects" like other beings have.

**Explanation:**

Ordinary things are composed of parts working together — a body made of organs, or even an abstract composition like a nature plus an individual instance of that nature. Aquinas argues God can't be like that in any respect: no physical parts (God isn't a body), no split between "what God is" and "the fact that God exists" (unlike everything else, discussed in a separate card), and no mixture of unrealized potential alongside actuality. This total lack of composition is what Aquinas means by divine simplicity, and he treats it as the foundation from which many of God's other traits — like being unchangeable and eternal — follow.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.3 (whole question)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("Divine Attributes and Nature")

**Status:** draft

### God's Essence Is God's Existence

**Claim:**

Unlike every created thing, God's very nature simply is to exist — in God there's no gap between "what God is" and "that God is."

**Explanation:**

For any creature, you can ask "what is it?" (its essence) as a separate question from "does it actually exist?" — nothing about being a unicorn guarantees any unicorns exist. Aquinas argues God is the one case where this gap collapses entirely: God's nature just is existence itself, with nothing added. It's a bit like the difference between a blueprint for a house (which could exist without any house being built) and something whose "blueprint," so to speak, is simply "to be real." This is why Aquinas thinks God's existence is necessary rather than contingent — there's nothing else needed to make God actual, because being actual is what God's nature already consists in.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.3, a.4
- Aquinas, De Ente et Essentia, ch. 4

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("Essence and Existence in God"); IEP: Aquinas, §5 Metaphysics

**Status:** draft

### Analogical God-Talk: Neither the Same Meaning Nor No Meaning

**Claim:**

When we call God "good," Aquinas holds the word means something genuinely related to human goodness, yet not exactly the same thing either.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas faces a puzzle: if "good" means for God exactly what it means for humans, we're cutting God down to a human scale; but if it means something totally unrelated, then calling God "good" is empty noise that tells us nothing. His solution is a middle path he calls analogy: human goodness is a limited, creaturely reflection of a goodness that exists in God in a vastly fuller, higher way — related, not identical, and not unrelated either. So the claim "God is good" is genuinely informative and true, even though we can't fully grasp what divine goodness is like in itself, only that our own goodness is a faint echo of it.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.13, a.5

**Misreadings (optional):**

This is not the same as saying "good" just means "God is not bad" (pure negation) or "God causes goodness in us" (pure causal talk) — Aquinas insists goodness genuinely, positively belongs to God, just in a higher mode than we can fully picture.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("Analogical Predication and Divine Language"); IEP: Aquinas, §3 Philosophy of Language: Analogy

**Status:** draft

### Natural Theology Has Limits: Some Truths Require Revelation

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that unaided reason can prove God exists and has many attributes, but cannot prove specifically Christian doctrines like the Trinity.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas draws a clear line between two kinds of religious claims. Some — like "God exists" or "there is only one God" — he thinks philosophy can strictly prove, no faith required; he calls these "preambles" to faith. Others — like the Trinity (three persons in one God) or the Incarnation (God becoming human in Jesus) — he holds are simply beyond what unaided human reasoning could ever demonstrate; these require revelation and are accepted on faith. He doesn't see this as a defeat for reason; he thinks it's exactly what we should expect, since some truths about an infinite God are simply too far above ordinary human capacities to reach through argument alone.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.1, a.1
- Summa Contra Gentiles (SCG) I, ch. 3

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §2 God ("Limits of Natural Theology"); IEP: Aquinas, §2 Faith and Reason ("preambles" vs. "articles" of faith)

**Status:** draft

### Faith and Reason Cannot Genuinely Conflict

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that any apparent contradiction between a proven philosophical truth and a genuine article of religious faith must be only apparent, not real.

**Explanation:**

Since Aquinas thinks both reason and revelation ultimately come from the same source — reality and God — he holds that a real, solid piece of philosophical reasoning can never actually contradict a genuine truth of faith. If they seem to clash, he says, either the "proof" wasn't actually solid, or the supposed article of faith was misunderstood. This means Aquinas doesn't treat philosophy as a threat to be defended against; he treats good philosophy as something faith has nothing to fear from, while also warning that using weak or bad arguments to "defend" faith actually damages its credibility.

**Citations:**
- SCG I, ch. 7

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §2 Faith and Reason; SEP: Aquinas, §3 The Created World ("The Eternity Question and Philosophical Limits")

**Status:** draft

### Creation Is Ongoing: God Continuously Sustains the World

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that God's act of creating isn't a one-time event in the past — the world's existence depends on God moment to moment, right now.

**Explanation:**

It's tempting to picture creation like a craftsman who builds something and then walks away, leaving it to run on its own. Aquinas rejects that picture. He argues that a thing's existence can't outlast the action that's causing it to exist — so if God's creative causing ever simply stopped, the universe wouldn't coast along on its own momentum, it would immediately stop existing altogether. This means every single moment the world exists, it's because God is, right then, actively holding it in being — a relationship Aquinas calls conservation, not a separate act from creation but its continuation.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.104, a.1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §3 The Created World ("Conservation and Divine Providence")

**Status:** draft

### God Creates Freely — There Is No "Best Possible World"

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that God was not compelled to create anything, and could always have made something even better than whatever God actually made.

**Explanation:**

Some philosophers have argued that a perfectly good God would have to create the single best possible universe. Aquinas disagrees on both counts. First, he holds creation itself is entirely optional for God — God's goodness is already complete without needing to produce anything at all, so nothing forces God's hand. Second, he holds there's no such thing as "the best possible world" to begin with, since for any created thing, a better version is always conceivable; the list of possible improvements never bottoms out. So God's choice to create this particular world is a genuinely free choice, not the forced output of a "maximize goodness" calculation.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.25, a.6

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §3 The Created World ("Freedom and Necessity in Creation")

**Status:** draft

### Whether the World Had a Beginning Cannot Be Proven by Reason Alone

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that pure philosophical argument cannot settle whether the universe has always existed or had a first moment — only faith settles it.

**Explanation:**

Many of Aquinas's contemporaries argued you could philosophically prove the world must have had a beginning, others that it must be eternal. Aquinas takes the more cautious middle position: he thinks no strictly logical argument works either way, and that a rational thinker with no access to revelation genuinely couldn't determine the answer through reason alone. He personally holds, on the authority of Christian revelation, that the world did have a beginning — but he's careful to insist this is something we know only because God told us, not something philosophy by itself can nail down.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.46, a.1–2
- Aquinas, De Aeternitate Mundi

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §3 The Created World ("The Eternity Question and Philosophical Limits")

**Status:** draft

## Soul, Mind, and Free Will

### The Soul Is the Form of the Body

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that the soul is not a separate ghost-like thing living inside the body, but the organizing principle that makes a body alive at all.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas doesn't picture the soul as a separate little person hiding inside you, the way popular imagination often does. Instead, following Aristotle, he treats "soul" as the name for whatever it is that makes a living thing count as alive and as the particular kind of living thing it is — the same basic role that a shape or structure plays in making a chunk of wood into a chair. Plants have souls that account for their nutrition and growth, animals have souls that also account for movement and sensing, and humans have souls that also account for reasoning. On this view, having "a soul" doesn't yet imply anything spiritual or immaterial — that further claim, for humans specifically, needs separate argument.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.76, a.1

**Misreadings (optional):**

Calling something "ensouled" in this Aristotelian sense doesn't mean it's spiritual or has an afterlife — plants and animals have souls in Aquinas's sense too, but (unlike humans) their souls don't survive bodily death.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §5 Soul and Body ("The Soul as Form of Living Things")

**Status:** draft

---

### The Human Soul Is Both a Body's Form and Something That Can Exist on Its Own

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that the human soul is unusual: it organizes the body like other souls do, yet can also exist and function independently of the body.

**Explanation:**

For plants and animals, Aquinas thinks the soul cannot exist apart from the body it organizes — it's not the kind of thing that survives on its own. Humans are the exception. Because human thinking involves grasping abstract, universal ideas (not just physical images), Aquinas argues this activity can't be fully explained by any bodily organ working alone — it must come from something not entirely dependent on matter. So the human soul plays two roles at once: it's the form that makes a human body a living, human body, and it's also a "subsistent" thing — something capable, unlike other souls, of existing and operating (in a limited way) even without the body.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.75, a.2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §5 Soul and Body ("The Immateriality of Human Intellect")

**Status:** draft

### The Human Soul Is Immortal

**Claim:**

Aquinas argues that because the human soul can exist independently of matter, it has no built-in tendency to fall apart, and so cannot naturally cease to exist.

**Explanation:**

Things typically stop existing when they break down into their component parts — the way a body decomposes once it dies. Aquinas argues that this kind of ending only applies to things made of parts held together, which is exactly what the human soul, as something not dependent on matter for its existence, is not. Since the soul has no parts to come apart, there's no natural process by which it could cease to be. Once God creates a human soul, Aquinas concludes, that soul continues existing forever — this is his philosophical argument for the soul's immortality, offered alongside (not instead of) the Christian doctrine of an afterlife.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.75, a.6

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §5 Soul and Body ("Immortality and the Separated Soul")

**Status:** draft

### Debate: Is a Disembodied Soul Alone Enough to Be "You"?

**Claim:**

Scholars disagree on whether Aquinas thinks the soul alone, without a body, still counts as the full person after death.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas holds the soul survives death, but he also says some striking things suggesting a soul by itself isn't the complete person — for instance, that a soul having life wouldn't be enough for the person it belonged to (like Abraham) to count as truly alive; the full living person needs soul and body together. Some scholars ("corruptionists") take this to mean that between death and the future bodily resurrection, "you" don't fully exist — only your soul does, as a kind of leftover part. Others ("survivalists") argue this can't be right, since Aquinas clearly holds the soul is immortal and identifies it closely enough with the person to keep thinking and willing. The texts genuinely underdetermine which reading is correct.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians, ch. 15 (exact locus of the "Abraham's soul" remark; precise numbering varies by edition)

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §5 Soul and Body ("Immortality and the Separated Soul" — corruptionist/survivalist debate)

**Status:** draft

### Will and Free Choice Are the Same Power, Not Two

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that "the will" and "free choice" are not two separate mental faculties, but one and the same power looked at differently.

**Explanation:**

It might seem natural to think there's a general faculty of "wanting things" (the will) and a separate faculty for "choosing freely among options" (free choice). Aquinas denies this: he argues they're the very same underlying power of the soul, just described from two angles. "Will" names this power in terms of its basic job — going after things that look good. "Free choice" names the same power in terms of how it operates when weighing particular options against each other. This matters because it means, for Aquinas, that freedom isn't some extra faculty bolted onto desire — freedom is simply built into how wanting itself works in a rational creature.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.83, a.4

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §7 Will and Freedom ("The Will as a Faculty of the Soul")

**Status:** draft

### The Intellect Proposes, the Will Chooses

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that every act of choosing is set in motion by the intellect judging something to be good, though the will isn't just a passive follower.

**Explanation:**

For Aquinas, wanting anything always starts with understanding: before you can choose something, some part of your mind has to size it up and judge "this looks good" or "this is worth pursuing." That judgment comes from the intellect, and the will can't just conjure its own reasons for acting out of nowhere — it needs the intellect's input to have anything to want. But Aquinas insists this doesn't make the will a mere puppet of the intellect: the will can redirect attention, ask the intellect to look again, or focus on a different angle of the same situation, so the relationship runs both ways rather than the intellect simply dictating outcomes.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.9, a.1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §7 Will and Freedom ("The Relationship Between Intellect and Will")

**Status:** draft

### We Necessarily Want Happiness, But Not Any Specific Path to It

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that everyone unavoidably wants to be happy, yet this doesn't force any particular real-life choice about how to pursue it.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas thinks it's simply built into being a rational creature that you want your own flourishing, overall well-being — nobody can just decide not to want to be happy in some general sense; that's a fixed feature of human nature, not a free choice. But this generic, built-in desire for happiness doesn't determine any specific decision, because any real-world option can be looked at from more than one angle — a hard workout can seem appealing (good for health) or unappealing (exhausting) depending which feature you focus on. The will controls which angle gets attention, which is where Aquinas locates genuine freedom, even though the deep-down desire for happiness itself is not up for grabs.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.10, a.1–2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §7 Will and Freedom ("Natural Desire and Constrained Freedom")

**Status:** draft

---

### The Will Moves Itself and Isn't Externally Forced

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that the will is a self-mover: unlike a rock pushed by an outside force, it determines its own activity from within.

**Explanation:**

For non-living things, whatever happens to them is entirely driven by outside causes — a rock doesn't decide to roll downhill, gravity and the slope do all the causal work. Aquinas argues the human will is different in kind: it moves itself, in the sense that a person can choose to exercise one of their own powers (like deciding, right now, to start thinking about something) rather than being simply triggered from outside. This self-moving feature is central to why Aquinas insists so strongly that the will cannot be strictly forced or necessitated by outside factors in the way natural objects are — he calls the opposite view a threat to both good ethics and religious belief in human responsibility.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Malo, Q.6

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §7 Will and Freedom ("The Self-Moving Will and Libertarian Readings")

**Status:** draft

---

### Debate: Does God's Providence Undercut Human Freedom?

**Claim:**

Scholars disagree on whether Aquinas's view that God orders all events is fully compatible with, or actually in tension with, genuine human free choice.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas wants to hold two things at once: God's plan encompasses and orders literally everything that happens, and human choices are still genuinely free rather than forced. He explicitly denies that divine ordering "takes away" the freedom of a human choice — he thinks God can cause an act to happen through a created cause while still leaving that cause free, because God's causing operates at a different level than one creature pushing around another. Some interpreters read this as a coherent, if unusual, form of compatibilism — freedom and total divine determination coexisting. Others think Aquinas's texts pull in a more libertarian direction, treating the will as an genuinely undetermined source of its own acts. Aquinas himself never fully resolves which reading is correct.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.22, a.4
- ST I, Q.83, a.1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §7 Will and Freedom ("The Determinism Debate")

**Status:** draft

## Epistemology (Knowledge and Mind)

### All Human Knowledge Starts With the Senses

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that every concept a human mind possesses ultimately traces back to information first taken in through the five senses.

**Explanation:**

Unlike thinkers who believe humans are born with ready-made abstract ideas already in the mind, Aquinas holds that the human intellect starts out completely empty and has to build up all of its concepts from raw sensory experience. Even highly abstract ideas — like "justice" or "cause" — ultimately get constructed by the mind working on material that first came in through seeing, hearing, touching, and so on. This doesn't mean abstract thought just is sensation; Aquinas thinks the intellect does real, transformative work on sense data (see the "abstraction" card), but that work always has to start from sensory raw material, with nothing bypassing the senses entirely.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.84, a.6

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.2 Cognitive Theory ("Thought"); IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology

**Status:** draft

---

### Abstraction: Turning Particular Sense-Images Into General Ideas

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that the intellect forms general concepts by actively stripping away particular, material details from sensory images.

**Explanation:**

When you see one particular dog, your senses only ever register this specific dog, with its specific size, color, and location. Yet somehow you also end up with a general concept of "dog" that applies to any dog whatsoever. Aquinas says this happens through a process he calls abstraction: a power of the mind he calls the "agent intellect" works on the mental images left over from sense experience (he calls these "phantasms") and pulls out the general, non-material pattern common to the individual instances, leaving behind what's just particular to this one case. The result is a genuinely universal concept — usable for any dog — even though it was built starting from one particular experience.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.85, a.1

**Misreadings (optional):**

Aquinas himself acknowledges this process is not fully transparent — exactly how stripping away material details from a particular image produces a genuinely universal concept remains one of the murkier parts of his theory, not a tidy mechanical recipe.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.2 Cognitive Theory ("Thought")

**Status:** draft

---

### Each Person Has Their Own Individual Intellect

**Claim:**

Aquinas rejects the view that all humans share one single collective intellect, insisting each person has their own separate power of thought.

**Explanation:**

Some philosophers in Aquinas's era, following the Muslim philosopher Ibn Rushd (known in Latin as Averroes), argued that the power making abstract thought possible isn't really individual to each person — it's one single shared intellect that all human bodies somehow tap into, like many lamps plugged into one power source. Aquinas argues forcefully against this. If thinking really happened in one shared intellect rather than in each person individually, he says, then it would be impossible to explain why different people think different things, learn at different rates, or hold different beliefs — your thoughts would really be "the group's" thoughts, not distinctly yours. So Aquinas insists each human being has their own complete, individual intellect.

**Citations:**
- Aquinas, De Unitate Intellectus Contra Averroistas

**Misreadings (optional):**

This debate is not about brains as physical organs (which obviously differ person to person) — it's about the immaterial intellectual power some contemporaries thought was literally numerically one thing shared across all human beings.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.2 Cognitive Theory ("Thought"); IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology

**Status:** draft

---

### The Interior Senses: More Than the Five External Senses

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that besides sight, hearing, smell, taste, and touch, animals and humans have four additional internal sensory powers.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas thinks the five familiar senses aren't the whole sensory story. He adds four "interior" senses, located (he thinks) in the brain rather than at the body's surface: a "common sense" that combines input from the different external senses into one unified experience and lets you notice that you're sensing at all; imagination, which stores and can recall sensory images even when the object is no longer present; an "estimative" power, which lets an animal instinctively size up something as dangerous or beneficial without reasoning it out (a sheep instinctively sensing a wolf as a threat); and memory, which stores those instinctive evaluations over time.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.78, a.4

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.1 Cognitive Theory ("Perception"); IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology ("The Sources of Knowledge: Philosophical Psychology")

**Status:** draft

---

### Scientia: A Demanding Standard for What Counts as "Real Knowledge"

**Claim:**

Aquinas reserves the label of genuine scientific knowledge for claims that are certain, causally explained, and logically demonstrated from self-evident starting points.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas uses a much stricter standard for "real knowledge" (what he calls scientia) than we usually do today. It's not enough for a belief to be true or even well-supported — for Aquinas, genuine scientific knowledge means you understand exactly why something is true, you're certain of it (not just confident), and you can trace it, through valid logical steps, back to starting principles that are self-evident and don't themselves need proof. Because of how demanding this bar is, Aquinas actually treats it as more of an ideal that disciplines aim at than something we usually fully achieve — plenty of ordinary human understanding, in his own view, falls short of strict scientia.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.1, a.2
- Aquinas, Commentary on Aristotle's Posterior Analytics, Bk. I

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.3 Cognitive Theory ("Knowledge and Science"); IEP: Aquinas, §4 Epistemology ("The Nature of Knowledge and Science")

**Status:** draft

---

### Sense Perception Takes In a Quality Without Taking On Its Matter

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that perceiving a quality like heat is not the same as your sense organ literally becoming hot the way the object is.

**Explanation:**

When you touch something hot, your skin might genuinely warm up a little — but Aquinas argues that the perceiving itself is a different kind of event from that physical heating. Your sense organ receives the "form" of heat — a kind of representation of hotness — without becoming hot in the same way the object is; otherwise, perceiving something freezing cold would require your hand to literally freeze. Aquinas describes this as receiving the quality "immaterially," meaning the sense takes in information about the quality without undergoing the same physical change the object underwent. Scholars still debate exactly how directly this connects the perceiver to the external object.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.78, a.3

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.1 Cognitive Theory ("Perception")

**Status:** draft

---

### Three Kinds of Sensible Qualities

**Claim:**

Aquinas sorts what the senses detect into three categories: qualities tied to one sense, qualities shared across senses, and qualities requiring judgment.

**Explanation:**

Some qualities are detected by exactly one sense and no other — color only by sight, sound only by hearing; Aquinas calls these "proper" sensibles. Other qualities, like size, shape, number, and motion, can be picked up by more than one sense at once — you can feel a ball's roundness by touch or see it by sight; these are "common" sensibles. Finally, some things get noticed "incidentally" alongside perception, but really involve a bit of judgment layered on top of raw sensing — like recognizing that the moving thing you're looking at is your friend, or that it's alive; Aquinas calls these "accidental" (incidental) sensibles.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.78, a.3

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §6.1 Cognitive Theory ("Perception")

**Status:** draft

## Ethics: Happiness, Natural Law, and Virtue

### Happiness Is Ultimately Contemplating God

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that complete human happiness, our final goal, consists in directly understanding God, not in pleasure, wealth, or worldly achievement.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas asks what could possibly satisfy a human being completely, with nothing left to want. He argues it can't be money, fame, pleasure, or even ordinary knowledge, because none of these ever fully satisfy — people who get them typically want more, or something different. He concludes that only directly grasping the ultimate source and explanation of everything — God — could ever fully complete a mind built to understand reality. In this present life, he thinks the closest approximation is a life devoted to contemplating truth; complete happiness, in the fullest sense, is something he thinks is only reachable after death.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.3, a.8

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.1 Ethics ("Happiness")

**Status:** draft

---

### Aquinas's Happiness-Based Ethics Isn't Actually Selfish

**Claim:**

Grounding ethics in the pursuit of one's own happiness does not, for Aquinas, make his ethical theory self-centered or opposed to loving others.

**Explanation:**

It can sound like building morality around "your own happiness" must be a selfish framework, at odds with loving God and other people for their own sake. Aquinas rejects that inference. On his view, what actually completes and fulfills a person is understanding and loving God — and genuinely understanding God, he thinks, naturally produces real love for God and for other people, since everyone reflects God's goodness in some way. So pursuing your own deepest fulfillment and genuinely loving others aren't competitors on Aquinas's view; he thinks they're the same underlying trajectory, described from two different angles.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.3, a.8
- ST II-II, Q.25, a.1 (on the connection between love of God and love of neighbor)

**Misreadings (optional):**

People sometimes assume "eudaimonism" (grounding ethics in happiness/flourishing) is automatically selfish; Aquinas's version explicitly builds other-directed love into what happiness itself consists in.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.1 Ethics ("Happiness")

**Status:** draft

---

### Four Kinds of Law

**Claim:**

Aquinas distinguishes four types of law: God's overall plan, its imprint on human reason, revealed divine commands, and human-made legislation.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas uses "law" more broadly than we usually do today, and sorts it into four kinds. "Eternal law" is God's overarching rational plan for ordering the entire universe. "Natural law" is how that plan shows up specifically in human beings, through our capacity to use reason to figure out what's genuinely good for us. "Divine law" is additional guidance God directly reveals (like the commandments in scripture), covering things reason alone might miss or get wrong. "Human law" is law that actual governments and societies write, which Aquinas thinks only counts as genuine, binding law when it's a reasonable application of natural law to particular circumstances.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.91

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law"); IEP: Aquinas, §9 Political Philosophy ("Law and Natural Law")

**Status:** draft

---

### Natural Law's First Principle: Do Good, Avoid Evil

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that all of morality ultimately rests on one self-evident first principle: pursue what is good, and avoid what is bad.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas thinks moral reasoning needs a starting point that doesn't itself need to be proved — otherwise you'd need an argument for your argument, forever. He proposes that the most basic principle of practical reasoning (reasoning about what to do, as opposed to reasoning about what's true) is simply: good is to be sought and done, and bad is to be avoided. This sounds almost too obvious to be useful, but Aquinas treats it as the foundation everything else in his ethical system builds on — more specific moral rules are, for him, ultimately applications of this one basic principle to particular kinds of human activity and circumstance.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.94, a.2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law")

**Status:** draft

---

### Synderesis: Our Built-In Grasp of Basic Moral Principles

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that humans have a natural, non-taught capacity to immediately recognize the most basic principles of right and wrong.

**Explanation:**

Just as Aquinas thinks the mind grasps certain basic logical truths immediately, without needing to be taught or argued into them (like "the same thing can't both be true and false"), he thinks there's a parallel capacity for grasping the most basic moral truths just as directly — he calls this capacity synderesis. It's not a separate mysterious faculty bolted onto the intellect; it's just the intellect exercising its natural grasp of first principles, applied to practical matters instead of theoretical ones. This gives Aquinas's ethics a foundation that doesn't depend on cultural teaching for its most basic starting points, even though applying those starting points to real situations still takes reasoning and can go wrong.

**Citations:**
- ST I, Q.79, a.12

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law")

**Status:** draft

---

### Natural Law Tracks Our Built-In Human Inclinations

**Claim:**

Aquinas derives specific moral precepts from natural human inclinations, such as self-preservation, raising children, and living in community.

**Explanation:**

Beyond the single most basic principle ("do good, avoid evil"), Aquinas thinks we can identify more specific moral guidance by noticing what humans are naturally inclined toward simply by being the kind of creature we are. We're naturally inclined to preserve our own lives (giving rise to precepts against things like reckless self-harm), to reproduce and raise children (giving rise to precepts about family life), to live socially with others (giving rise to precepts about honesty and cooperation), and to seek to understand truth, including truth about God. For Aquinas these inclinations aren't just biological urges to satisfy — they point toward what actually makes human beings flourish, so reasoning about them yields genuine moral guidance.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.94, a.2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law")

**Status:** draft

---

### Human Law Is Only Truly Binding When It Reflects Natural Law

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that a human-made law that contradicts natural law fails to be genuine law at all, whatever a government calls it.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas doesn't think "the government said so" is enough, by itself, to make something a real, binding law. He argues human laws only carry genuine moral authority when they're reasonable applications of natural law — that is, when they actually track what's good for the community rather than just imposing someone's arbitrary will. A rule that clearly violates natural law (say, one that's flatly unjust or serves only a ruler's private interest) is, for Aquinas, not really law in the fullest sense at all, even if it's formally enacted and enforced — it's closer to an act of coercion dressed up as law.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.95, a.2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law"); IEP: Aquinas, §9 Political Philosophy

**Status:** draft

### Natural Law Isn't the Same Thing as Modern "Natural Rights"

**Claim:**

Aquinas's "natural law," which describes reason grasping objective human goods, is a different idea from later natural-rights talk about individual entitlements.

**Explanation:**

Modern political language often uses "natural rights" to mean claims individuals can make against others or the state — inherent entitlements a person holds simply by being human. Aquinas's "natural law" is an older and different idea: it's about reason recognizing objective goods built into human nature (like life, knowledge, and community) and drawing out moral obligations from them — it's fundamentally about what's good and what we owe, not primarily about individual entitlements or claims. Reading later rights-based political theory back into Aquinas's natural law, or assuming they're doing the same conceptual work, is a common but importantly mistaken anachronism.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.94 (natural law generally, as contrasted with later natural-rights theory)

**Misreadings (optional):**

This card is itself flagging the misreading: treating Aquinas as an early theorist of individual natural rights conflates two related but historically and philosophically distinct traditions.

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.2 Ethics ("Natural Law" — on grounding normativity in facts about human nature)

**Status:** draft

### Virtue Is a Reliable Good Habit, Not Just a Skill or a One-Off Good Deed

**Claim:**

Aquinas defines virtue as a stable character trait that reliably produces good action, sharply distinct from a skill or an isolated good deed.

**Explanation:**

Doing one kind thing doesn't make you a kind person, and being skilled at something (like carpentry) doesn't automatically make you good — a skilled carpenter can still build something for a bad purpose. Aquinas defines virtue as a settled, durable quality of a person's mind or character that consistently and reliably produces genuinely good action, not just a one-off performance or a technical ability that could be used well or badly. Crucially, he holds real virtue is incapable of producing a bad action — if a supposed "virtue" sometimes leads to genuinely bad behavior, that's evidence it wasn't full virtue at all, just something that resembled it.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.55, a.4

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Ethics ("Virtue Theory")

### The Four Cardinal Virtues Each Perfect a Different Part of the Person

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that prudence, justice, courage, and temperance are basic virtues, each perfecting a distinct power involved in human action.

**Explanation:**

Aquinas organizes the traditional four "cardinal" (meaning hinge-like, foundational) virtues around different parts of a person's psychology. Prudence perfects practical reason itself — the ability to figure out the right thing to do in a specific situation. Justice perfects the will as it relates to other people — giving each person what they're rightly owed. Courage (or fortitude) perfects our capacity to face fear and danger without being controlled by it. Temperance perfects our desire for pleasure, keeping bodily appetites in line with reason rather than running the show. Aquinas treats prudence as foundational among them, since none of the others functions well without practical wisdom guiding it.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.61, a.2

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Ethics ("Virtue Theory")

**Status:** draft

---

### The Unity of the Virtues: You Can't Have Just One

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that a person cannot possess one cardinal virtue fully without possessing all the others as well.

**Explanation:**

It might seem like someone could be genuinely courageous while being unjust, or temperate while being cowardly. Aquinas denies this is possible in the full sense of virtue. He argues that each cardinal virtue depends on practical wisdom (prudence) to actually operate well, and prudence itself requires drawing on all the other virtues to function properly in real situations — so the virtues end up interlocking rather than standing alone. Someone might have isolated good habits or tendencies in one area while lacking others, but Aquinas would say that's not the same as possessing genuine, complete virtue, which for him always comes as an interconnected package.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.65, a.1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Ethics ("Virtue Theory")

### Theological Virtues Are Gifts of Grace, Not Things You Can Earn

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that faith, hope, and charity are virtues directly given by God, not skills a person can build up through practice alone.

**Explanation:**

The four cardinal virtues (prudence, justice, courage, temperance) can be built up gradually through practice and habituation, the way you'd build any character trait. Aquinas holds that three further virtues — faith (trusting God's revealed truth), hope (confidently desiring a difficult, God-given future good), and charity (genuinely loving God and others for God's own sake) — work differently: they're directly given ("infused") by God rather than developed through repeated human effort. Aquinas thinks these are necessary because they aim at a goal — full union with God — that lies beyond what unaided human effort or ordinary virtue-building could ever reach on its own.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.62, a.1

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Ethics ("Virtue Theory")

### Virtuous Action Is Genuinely Pleasurable, Not Grim Duty

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that acting virtuously is inherently satisfying and enjoyable for the virtuous person, not a matter of gritted-teeth obligation.

**Explanation:**

There's a common caricature of Aquinas's ethics as demanding joyless self-denial, doing the right thing purely out of duty regardless of how it feels. Aquinas actually rejects this picture. He holds that once virtue is genuinely established as a stable character trait, acting on it becomes something the virtuous person takes real pleasure in — like how an experienced pianist enjoys playing well, rather than gritting their teeth through it, once the skill has become second nature. For Aquinas, needing constant willpower to resist temptation toward bad action is actually a sign that full virtue hasn't been achieved yet, not the model of what fully virtuous living looks like.

**Citations:**
- ST I-II, Q.59, a.2
- Aquinas, Sententia Libri Ethicorum (Commentary on Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics) — general reference; exact book/lecture not confirmed

**Misreadings (optional):**

Aquinas is often assumed to teach a grim, rule-bound, pleasure-suspicious ethics; he explicitly holds the opposite about what mature virtue feels like from the inside.

**Provenance:** IEP: Aquinas, §8 Ethics ("Virtuous Action and Happiness")

**Status:** draft

### Prudence Is the Master Virtue That Applies Principles to Real Situations

**Claim:**

Aquinas holds that prudence, or practical wisdom, is required for every other virtue to actually produce good action in real circumstances.

**Explanation:**

Knowing general moral principles ("be generous," "be brave") isn't enough to actually act well, because real situations are always specific and messy — how much to give, to whom, under what circumstances, requires judgment, not just a rule to apply mechanically. Aquinas calls the virtue responsible for this kind of situational judgment prudence: the practical skill of correctly figuring out the right thing to do here and now, and then actually doing it. He treats prudence as indispensable glue holding the other virtues together — courage without prudence risks becoming recklessness, and generosity without prudence risks becoming wastefulness, so genuinely good character always requires prudence operating alongside the other virtues.

**Citations:**
- ST II-II, Q.47, a.6–8

**Provenance:** SEP: Aquinas, §8.3 Ethics ("Virtue Theory" — prudence as foundational); IEP: Aquinas, §9 Political Philosophy ("prudence... mediates between universal principles and particular situations")

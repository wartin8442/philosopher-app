panel. Each entry names one titled section of one work and says, in our own

## The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

### The Transcendence of the Ego — I.A: The Theory of the Formal Presence of the I

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part I "The I and the Me", §A "The Theory of the Formal Presence of the I"

**Summary:** Sartre grants Kant that it must be *possible* for the "I think" to accompany each of our representations, but argues this is a claim about validity, not a claim that an I actually inhabits every act of consciousness. Turning to Husserl, he accepts a constituting consciousness and treats the psycho-physical "me" as a transcendent object, but asks whether a transcendental I must be added behind it, and answers no. Consciousness unifies itself through the objects it intends and through retentions of its own past, and its individuality follows from its own nature, so a unifying I is useless. Worse, it would be harmful: an opaque centre lodged in consciousness that would split it from itself. Ordinary, unreflected consciousness is aware of itself only non-positionally, and there is no I in it.

**Key terms:** transcendental I; "I think"; Husserl's *Ideas*; intentionality; unreflected (first-degree) consciousness; non-positional self-awareness

### The Transcendence of the Ego — I.B: The Cogito as Reflective Consciousness

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part I "The I and the Me", §B "The Cogito as Reflective Consciousness"

**Summary:** Sartre grants that the Cogito of Descartes and Husserl is personal, but argues it is a reflective, second-degree act, and the reflecting consciousness itself contains no I. Consulting the non-thetic memory of unreflected moments (absorbed in a novel, running after a streetcar), he finds consciousness of objects and no I; Titchener noticed the same. Reflection does not grasp the I as a concrete moment of consciousness: the I offers itself as permanent, nearer to eternal truths, through the reflected consciousness, with neither apodictic nor adequate evidence, veiled like a pebble seen through water. Descartes slid from the Cogito to a thinking substance, and Husserl gives the I an unearned transcendence "from above". The I is therefore a transcendent object that appears only on reflection and must fall under the phenomenological reduction: the Cogito affirms too much.

**Key terms:** Cogito; reflective vs. unreflected consciousness; reflection; the streetcar example; apodictic and adequate evidence; Descartes's thinking substance; phenomenological reduction

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part I §B

### The Transcendence of the Ego — I.C: The Theory of the Material Presence of the Me

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part I "The I and the Me", §C "The Theory of the Material Presence of the Me"

**Summary:** Sartre turns against the "self-love" moralists, La Rochefoucauld above all, who hold that a hidden me, seeking its own satisfaction, lurks behind every desire. Their error is to read the structure of reflection into unreflected acts and then call it unconscious. When I pity Peter and go to help him, my consciousness meets only "Peter-having-to-be-helped", a quality of the world that draws me; there is no me in it, and an unpleasant feeling I act to end could be known as mine only by reflection. Unreflected consciousness is autonomous and has ontological priority over reflection. The ego-life belongs to the reflective level: reflection "poisons" desire by making me watch myself act. The me appears only with reflection, and Sartre proposes that I and me are two aspects of one ego, unity of actions and unity of states.

**Key terms:** self-love; La Rochefoucauld; egoism and altruism; unreflected desire; reflection "poisons" desire; helping Peter; I and me

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part I §C

### The Transcendence of the Ego — II.A: States as Transcendent Unities of Consciousness

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part II "The Constitution of the Ego", §A "States as Transcendent Unities of Consciousness"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that a psychic state such as hatred is not part of consciousness but a transcendent object that reflection grasps through it. I cannot be wrong that Peter repels me now, but my hatred of Peter claims to have existed yesterday and to last tomorrow, so it overflows every momentary consciousness, much as a perceived inkstand exceeds each view of it; that I hate him therefore always remains doubtful. He distinguishes pure reflection, which keeps to what is given, from impure, "conniving" reflection, as when someone says "I detest you" in anger and then takes it back. States are inert and passive, which is why novelists like Balzac and Proust picture them as physical forces. Reflection presents the momentary consciousness as an emanation from the state, a bond Sartre calls magical rather than logical.

**Key terms:** psychic state; hatred of Peter; pure and impure reflection; transcendent object; passivity and inertia; emanation; magical bond

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part II §A

### The Transcendence of the Ego — II.B: The Constitution of Actions

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part II "The Constitution of the Ego", §B "The Constitution of Actions"

**Summary:** Sartre sets aside the hard question of how active consciousness differs from merely spontaneous consciousness, and argues only that a concerted action is a transcendent object. This is plain for actions carried out among things, such as playing the piano, driving a car, or writing, but it holds equally for purely mental actions like doubting, reasoning, or framing a hypothesis. An action takes time and has articulated moments, each matched by an active consciousness, and reflection grasps the whole action as the transcendent unity of those consciousnesses. So the sudden doubt I feel on glimpsing something in the shadows is a consciousness, while Descartes's methodical doubt is an action, and Sartre warns that "I doubt therefore I am" is ambiguous between the two.

**Key terms:** action; spontaneous consciousness; psychical actions; methodical doubt; Descartes; "I doubt therefore I am"

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part II §B

### The Transcendence of the Ego — II.C: Qualities as Optional Unities of States

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part II "The Constitution of the Ego", §C "Qualities as Optional Unities of States"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that a quality, such as being spiteful or ill-tempered, is a transcendent object, an optional intermediate unity of states and actions. When I have felt repeated hatreds, resentments, or angers, I unify them by intending a disposition that produces them, and this disposition underlies states as states underlie particular experiences. The quality relates to a state not by emanation but by actualization: it is a potentiality, really existing in the mode of potency, whose actuality is the state or action. Hence hatred is given as actually existing even when no consciousness of it is present, whereas spite with no feeling of spite remains only potential. Faults, virtues, tastes, talents, and instincts are of this kind; such unifications are shaped by preconceived ideas and social factors, and are optional, since states and actions can find their unity directly in the ego.

**Key terms:** quality; disposition; potentiality; actualization vs. emanation; virtues and faults; character traits

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part II §C

### The Transcendence of the Ego — II.D: The Constitution of the Ego as the Pole of Actions, States, and Qualities

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part II "The Constitution of the Ego", §D "The Constitution of the Ego as the Pole of Actions, States, and Qualities"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that the ego is a transcendent, psychic object that appears to reflection as the concrete totality of its states and actions, not an empty X-pole modeled on Husserl's object-pole; it stands to psychic objects as the World stands to things. The ego is given as magically creating its states, but this is a degraded pseudo-spontaneity projected onto it by consciousness, which Bergson mistook for freedom; hence surprises like "I could hate my father!" It is affected only by what it produces, as Raskolnikov's crime became a "killing bruise". Too intimate to view from outside, the me stays unknown: to know oneself is to take another's view, and the ego appears only "out of the corner of the eye". Unreflected speech keeps the I only as an empty concept, which the body falsely seems to fill.

**Key terms:** ego as transcendent object; Husserl's X-pole; the World analogy; poetic production; pseudo-spontaneity; magic and sorcery; Raskolnikov; self-knowledge; intimacy and indistinctness

**References:**

### The Transcendence of the Ego — II.E: The I and Consciousness in the Cogito

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Part II "The Constitution of the Ego", §E "The I and Consciousness in the Cogito"

**Summary:** Sartre asks why an I appears in the Cogito at all, since a correctly performed Cogito grasps pure consciousness and constitutes no states or actions. His answer is that the phenomenological reduction is never perfect: psychological motives intervene. Descartes performs the Cogito together with methodical doubt and the ambition to advance science, which are undertakings of an I, and the Cogito, given as both the result and the end of that doubt, sees an I appear on its horizon. The Cogito is therefore impure: it is bound up with the consciousness of states and actions. A reflection that met consciousness as non-personal spontaneity, with no prior motive, is possible in principle but rare in the human condition, and there no I is needed; in any case, the I that appears in the Cogito is not given as the producer of consciousness, which rather goes toward it.

**Key terms:** Cogito; impure Cogito; Descartes; methodical doubt; phenomenological reduction; non-personal spontaneity

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Part II §E

### The Transcendence of the Ego — Conclusion

**Work:** The Transcendence of the Ego (1936–37)

**Locator:** Conclusion

**Summary:** Sartre draws three consequences. First, the transcendental field, purged of the ego, becomes an impersonal spontaneity that creates itself each instant; since the me is an object, Peter's love is as accessible to Paul as to Peter, and only consciousness itself stays impenetrable. Rimbaud's "I is an other" states the point, and the ego's real function may be to mask consciousness's spontaneity from itself, as in Janet's young bride seized by a "vertigo of possibility"; the resulting anguish gives the phenomenological reduction the motive Fink found lacking. Second, this conception is the only refutation of solipsism, since my I is no more certain than others', only more intimate. Third, it answers the charge from the extreme Left that phenomenology is an idealism: a me contemporaneous with the World is enough to found a fully positive ethics and politics.

**Key terms:** impersonal spontaneity; transcendental field; other minds; "I is an other"; anguish; solipsism; idealism and realism

**References:**
- Primary text: The Transcendence of the Ego, trans. Forrest Williams and Robert Kirkpatrick (1957), Conclusion

## Being and Nothingness (1943)

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §I: The Phenomenon

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §I "The Phenomenon"

**Summary:** Sartre credits modern thought, and the phenomenology of Husserl and Heidegger, with treating an existent as nothing more than the series of its appearances, and so dissolving old dualisms: inner and outer (a force or an electric current is just the totality of its effects), being and appearance (no Kantian noumenon behind the phenomenon), potency and act (Proust's genius is his work, not a hidden capacity), and appearance and essence (essence becomes the principle of the series). But he argues these dualisms return as a new one, finite and infinite: since a subject can always multiply viewpoints, each finite appearance points to an infinite series that never itself appears, "the infinite in the finite." Because the appearance rests on no being other than its own, Sartre ends by posing the problem of the being of this appearing.

**Key terms:** phenomenon; appearance and essence; monism of the phenomenon; Kant's noumenon; finite and infinite; Abschattung; inexhaustibility

**References:**
- Primary text: Being and Nothingness, trans. Hazel E. Barnes (1956), Introduction, §I
- IEP, "Jean Paul Sartre: Existentialism", §3a "The Being of the Phenomenon and Consciousness" — https://iep.utm.edu/sartre-ex/

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §II: The Phenomenon of Being and the Being of the Phenomenon

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §II "The Phenomenon of Being and the Being of the Phenomenon"

**Summary:** Sartre grants that being shows itself through some immediate access, such as boredom or nausea, so there is a phenomenon of being. But he asks whether this phenomenon of being is the same as the being of phenomena, and argues it is not. Husserl's eidetic reduction passes from a particular red to its essence, like to like; being, by contrast, is neither one of an object's qualities nor its meaning, since even absence discloses being. Being is "the condition of all revelation," not something revealed: when I turn to the being of this table, I am looking at a new appearance that itself needs a being to rest on. So the phenomenon of being, like the ontological proof of Anselm and Descartes, points to a transphenomenal being that is coextensive with the phenomenon yet exceeds and grounds our knowledge of it.

**Key terms:** phenomenon of being; being of the phenomenon; transphenomenal being; eidetic reduction; ontic-ontological; condition of all revelation

**References:**
- Primary text: Being and Nothingness, trans. Hazel E. Barnes (1956), Introduction, §II
- IEP, "Jean Paul Sartre: Existentialism", §3a "The Being of the Phenomenon and Consciousness" — https://iep.utm.edu/sartre-ex/

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §III: The Pre-Reflective Cogito and the Being of the Percipere

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §III "The Pre-Reflective Cogito and the Being of the Percipere"

**Summary:** Sartre rejects Berkeley's "esse est percipi," and Husserl's version of it, beginning with the perceiver: knowledge cannot ground its own being, so the knower's being must be transphenomenal, and that being is consciousness. Following Husserl, all consciousness is consciousness of something; it has no contents, and the table is in the world, not in the mind. Yet to know an object, consciousness must be aware of itself, and Sartre argues this awareness cannot be a knowledge of knowledge, as in Spinoza or Alain, without an infinite regress. Instead every positional consciousness of an object is a non-positional consciousness (of) itself: counting cigarettes, I attend to the dozen, yet if asked I at once answer "I am counting." This pre-reflective cogito is the condition of Descartes' reflective one. Consciousness, whose existence implies its essence, is a non-substantial absolute.

**Key terms:** pre-reflective cogito; non-positional (non-thetic) self-consciousness; intentionality; esse est percipi; reflection; consciousness (of) self; non-substantial absolute

**References:**
- Primary text: Being and Nothingness, trans. Hazel E. Barnes (1956), Introduction, §III

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §IV: The Being of the Percipi

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §IV "The Being of the Percipi"

**Summary:** Having found in consciousness a being that founds knowledge, Sartre asks whether consciousness can also supply the being of what it perceives, and argues it cannot. The perceived table is not my knowledge of it, so it needs a being of its own; if that being were merely its being-perceived, it would be passive and relative. But passivity is a relation between two existing beings, never a gift of being to nothing (even someone who stays "the one who has been offended" must assume that condition), and consciousness, as pure spontaneity, cannot act on anything to receive or give such passivity. Husserl's hyle, a passive stratum in consciousness, only creates a hybrid that is neither consciousness nor world. Relativity and passivity describe modes of being, not being; the error of the phenomenalists was to confuse the two.

**Key terms:** percipi; percipiens; esse est percipi; passivity; relativity; hyle; phenomenalism

**References:**
- Primary text: Being and Nothingness, trans. Hazel E. Barnes (1956), Introduction, §IV

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §V: The Ontological Proof

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §V "The Ontological Proof"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that the transphenomenal being of consciousness, far from replacing that of the phenomenon, requires it: an "ontological proof" drawn from the pre-reflective being of the perceiver rather than from the reflective cogito. If consciousness constituted its object, the object's objectivity would rest on absent, never-given appearances and so on non-being, and the objective can never come out of the subjective. Husserl defined consciousness as transcendence but betrayed this by making the noema unreal. Intentionality means consciousness is born supported by a being that is not itself; a pure subjectivity would vanish. Sartre distinguishes this from Kant's refutation of idealism, preferring Descartes, and amends Heidegger's definition of Dasein: consciousness is a being whose being is in question insofar as it implies a being other than itself. That being is the being of this table and lamp, not a noumenon.

**Key terms:** ontological proof; intentionality; transcendence; transphenomenal being; noema; subjectivity; Heidegger's Dasein

**References:**
- Primary text: Being and Nothingness, trans. Hazel E. Barnes (1956), Introduction, §V

### Being and Nothingness — Introduction, §VI: Being-in-Itself

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Introduction "The Pursuit of Being", §VI "Being-in-Itself"

**Summary:** Sartre draws provisional conclusions about the being of phenomena, which he calls being-in-itself and sets against the being of consciousness, being-for-itself. Having ruled out both realism and idealism, he leaves two regions of being apparently without communication. Against "creationism," including Leibniz and the doctrine of perpetual creation, he argues being is uncreated, yet not self-caused like consciousness. It is beyond activity and passivity, affirmation and negation. He fixes three characteristics: being is in itself, glued to itself with no distance from itself; being is what it is, a solid, opaque identity that admits no otherness, becoming, or negation; and being is, contingent, neither derived from the possible nor reducible to the necessary, and so "de trop." The section closes by listing the questions the book must answer, chief among them how these two regions are related.

**Key terms:** being-in-itself; being-for-itself; being is what it is; contingency; de trop; creationism; realism and idealism

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 1, Ch. 1, §I: The Question

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part One "The Problem of Nothingness", Chapter One "The Origin of Negation", §I "The Question"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that the Introduction reached an impasse because it separated two terms that are given together. Taking a cue from Descartes and from Laporte's and Husserl's notion of abstraction, he holds that consciousness and the phenomenon are each abstractions; the concrete is man in the world, Heidegger's "being-in-the-world." Kant's inquiry into the conditions of experience and Husserl's reduction both start from the abstract. As a guiding thread he takes one human conduct: questioning. Every question expects a yes or a no, and the negative reply ("Nothing," "Nobody," "Never") is always objectively possible; calling it a "fiction" only disguises the negation. So questioning involves a triple non-being: the questioner's not-knowing, the possible non-being in what is questioned, and the limitation implied by truth. Non-being thus appears as a new component of the real.

**Key terms:** the question; non-being; being-in-the-world; abstract and concrete; negative reply; triple non-being

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 1, Ch. 1, §II: Negations

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part One "The Problem of Nothingness", Chapter One "The Origin of Negation", §II "Negations"

**Summary:** Against the view that negation belongs to judgment alone, so that finding 1300 francs where I expected 1500 discloses no non-being, Sartre argues that nothingness, as a structure of the real, grounds negative judgment rather than the reverse. Non-being appears only against human expectation, but it is not merely subjective. Questioning is pre-judicative: I question a stalled carburetor, ready for "nothing there." Destruction likewise: a storm only redistributes masses, but man, by treating a city or a vase as fragile, lets non-being into things. The decisive example is Pierre's absence: arriving late at the café, I see the room fade into a ground against which Pierre's absence appears, a "double nihilation," whereas "Wellington is not in this café" is merely thought. Negation cannot come from positive judgments or a mental category; nothingness must haunt being.

**Key terms:** negation; negative judgment; nihilation; Pierre's absence; the café; destruction and fragility; figure and ground

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 3, Ch. 1, §II: The Reef of Solipsism

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Three "Being-for-Others", Chapter One "The Existence of Others", §II "The Reef of Solipsism"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that neither realism nor idealism can secure the Other. Realism separates souls by bodies, so the Other's mind is never intuited, only inferred by analogy, and the realist, admitting this knowledge is merely probable, slides into idealism. Kantian idealism fares no better: the Other can be neither a constitutive nor a regulative concept, since causality cannot bridge two separate times and the Other is the one for whom I am object. The idealist is left with solipsism (Watson's behaviorism as a working hypothesis) or an unjustified realism; Schopenhauer's jibe at the solipsist as a madman in a blockhouse betrays this helplessness. Leibniz's appeal to God tacitly relies on internal negation, but God is neither necessary nor sufficient; a positive theory must avoid solipsism without God.

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 3, Ch. 1, §IV: The Look

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Three "Being-for-Others", Chapter One "The Existence of Others", §IV "The Look"

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter One "Being and Doing: Freedom", §II "Freedom and Facticity: The Situation", subsection B "My Past"

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 1, §II.C: My Environment

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter One "Being and Doing: Freedom", §II "Freedom and Facticity: The Situation", subsection C "My Environment"

**Summary:** Sartre distinguishes my environment, the instrumental things around me with their adversity and utility, from my place: it can change without my doing anything. Cycling to the next town to see Pierre, I meet a flat tire, a head wind, a hot sun; the puncture may make me give up my plan, which looks like proof that freedom is limited. Sartre replies that such accidents become reasons to abandon a project only in the light of a more basic project, and that renouncing is itself freely constituted; even the torturer's red-hot pincers leave us free. Freedom in fact needs an indifferent world to act on: "to be free is to-be-free-to-change". Every project leaves a margin for the unforeseen, as the Romans kept a place for unknown gods. Hence I am absolutely free and responsible, but never free except in situation.

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 1, §II.D: My Fellowman

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter One "Being and Doing: Freedom", §II "Freedom and Facticity: The Situation", subsection D "My Fellowman"

**Summary:** Sartre asks whether a world already full of meanings set by others (street signs, "Entrance" and "Exit", collective techniques, a national language) limits my freedom. Mostly not: techniques exist only as individuals take them up and surpass them, as the sentence comes before the word and "it is by speaking that I make grammar". Nor did the Albigenses lack artillery, since they chose themselves in a world without it. The real limit is the Other's freedom, which makes me an object, so that I am Jew or Aryan, ugly or one-armed, without having chosen it. These "unrealizables" are real for others but cannot be lived from inside, like the returned exile who cannot feel he "is in Paris". Yet I must assume them, in pride, shame or refusal, and only my choice gives a prohibition like "No Jews allowed" its force. Freedom can be limited only by freedom.

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 1, §II.E: My Death

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter One "Being and Doing: Freedom", §II "Freedom and Facticity: The Situation", subsection E "My Death"

**Summary:** Sartre rejects the humanized death of Rilke, Malraux and Heidegger's being-toward-death, the resolved chord that gives life its meaning. Death is absurd: we are like a condemned man preparing for the scaffold who is carried off by flu. That nobody can die for me is no more special than that nobody can love for me. One can wait for a train from Chartres but not for death, which may come at any time; far from completing a life, it removes its meaning, as with a young writer dead after one book. It hands my life to others: "to be dead is to be a prey for the living". A contingent fact like birth, it is distinct from finitude (an immortal would still be finite): I am not "free to die" but a free mortal. The subsection ends with a summary of being-in-situation.

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 1, §III: Freedom and Responsibility

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter One "Being and Doing: Freedom", §III "Freedom and Responsibility"

**Summary:** Sartre, prefacing what follows as of interest above all to the ethicist, draws what he calls the essential consequence of his earlier remarks: being condemned to be free, a person carries the weight of the whole world and bears responsibility, as his very way of being, for the world and for himself, taking responsibility in its ordinary sense as "consciousness (of) being the incontestable author of an event or of an object." Complaint is senseless, and there is no non-human situation, even in war or torture. His example is war: if I am mobilized, it is my war, because I could have escaped by suicide or desertion; endorsing Jules Romains, there are "no innocent victims", and "we have the war we deserve". I am responsible for everything except my responsibility itself, since I did not lay the ground of my own existence; even "I did not ask to be born" is a way of assuming my birth. Realizing this brings anguish, without remorse, regret or excuse, though mostly we flee it in bad faith.

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 2, §I: Existential Psychoanalysis

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter Two "Doing and Having", §I "Existential Psychoanalysis"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that empirical psychology, illustrated by Bourget's account of the young Flaubert, builds a person out of general drives and halts at unexplained "givens" like ambition, so the person dissolves into a bundle of tendencies or an empty substance. Because a person is a totality expressed whole in each tendency, the analyst must compare desires (Flaubert's need to write, Pierre's fondness for rowing) until reaching a self-evidently irreducible original project. That project is the desire to be: the for-itself, as lack, aims at the in-itself-for-itself, so man is "the desire to be God", a meaning that never fixes any particular desire and so leaves freedom intact. Existential psychoanalysis borrows from Freud the view that conduct is symbolic and situated, but rejects the unconscious, libido or will to power as presupposed irreducibles, and any universal symbolism; Heidegger's authentic/inauthentic classification is likewise not fundamental enough.

**Key terms:** existential psychoanalysis; original choice; fundamental project; desire to be God; in-itself-for-itself; Freud and the unconscious; Flaubert

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 2, §II: "Doing" and "Having": Possession

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter Two "Doing and Having", §II "'Doing' and 'Having': Possession"

**Summary:** Sartre argues that concrete desire takes three forms, to do, to have and to be, and that doing always reduces to having or being: making a cane, creating a work of art, and knowing (the "Actaeon complex") are appropriations, while play, opposed to the spirit of seriousness, is a desire to be, though skiing still appropriates the snowfield by sliding. Possession is neither use nor legal right (Proudhon's "ownership is theft" begs the question) but an internal bond of continuous creation, so that "I am what I have." Because the owned object stays independent, possession is symbolic and never completed (the bicycle), which is why appropriation turns into destruction, consumption and generosity (the potlatch). Through one object we aim to possess the whole world, as Sartre's own struggle to quit smoking shows, so having and being are two routes to the single project of being God.

**Key terms:** to do, to have, to be; appropriation; possession; spirit of seriousness; play and sport; skiing and sliding; generosity as destruction

### Being and Nothingness — Pt. 4, Ch. 2, §III: Quality as a Revelation of Being

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Part Four "Having, Doing and Being", Chapter Two "Doing and Having", §III "Quality as a Revelation of Being"

**Summary:** Sartre calls for a psychoanalysis of things, praising Bachelard's work on water but faulting its talk of imagination and its borrowed Freudian and Adlerian postulates: the meaning of material qualities must be set by ontology, as modes in which being gives itself. Against the view that we project feelings onto matter, he notes that even young children recoil from the slimy before learning any moral analogy. His central example is the slimy (honey, pitch): it seems possessable but clings like a leech, so the possessor is possessed; it symbolizes the in-itself absorbing the for-itself, "the revenge of the In-itself", an antivalue. The child's urge to fill holes reveals a pre-sexual tendency to plenitude. Tastes and food preferences are therefore never irreducible but express a choice of being, and since the project of being God is contradictory, "man is a useless passion."

**Key terms:** the slimy (le visqueux); psychoanalysis of things; Bachelard; antivalue; holes and filling; taste and food; useless passion

### Being and Nothingness — Conclusion, §I: In-Itself and For-Itself: Metaphysical Implications

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Conclusion, §I "In-Itself and For-Itself: Metaphysical Implications"

**Summary:** Sartre answers the dualism feared in the Introduction: the for-itself is not a second substance but the nihilation of the in-itself, "a hole of being at the heart of Being", and the upheaval it causes in the in-itself is the world; he likens it to the "other" of Plato's *Sophist*. Why the for-itself arises is a question for metaphysics, which studies individual events, not for ontology, which can say only that everything happens as if the in-itself, in an attempt to be its own foundation, had modified itself into the for-itself. Being as a whole could be unified only as the ens causa sui, which is impossible, so reality is a failed synthesis, "a missing God", a detotalized totality that Sartre calls "decapitated", invoking the principle he credits to Spinoza and Hegel that a synthesis halted before completion becomes an error. Metaphysics must also decide whether the world is best treated as one phenomenon with two dimensions, on the model of Einstein's space-time.

**Key terms:** in-itself and for-itself; dualism; ens causa sui; detotalized totality; ontology versus metaphysics; Plato's Other; origin of the for-itself

### Being and Nothingness — Conclusion, §II: Ethical Implications

**Work:** Being and Nothingness (1943)

**Locator:** Conclusion, §II "Ethical Implications"

**Summary:** Sartre holds that ontology cannot issue ethical imperatives, since it describes only what is, but it reveals value as the lack that haunts the for-itself, making existential psychoanalysis a moral description that moves beyond utilitarian, egoist and altruist readings of conduct. Its main consequence should be our rejection of the spirit of seriousness, which treats values as given independently of us (bread is desirable because one must live) and is a form of bad faith. Those who grasp that they pursue being yet stay serious are condemned to despair, finding all activities equivalent: it comes to the same whether one gets drunk alone or leads nations. Freedom must instead discover, in anguish, that the agent is "the being by whom values exist." Whether freedom can take itself as its own value, and whether that is bad faith, Sartre leaves to a future ethical work.

**Key terms:** ethics and ontology; value; spirit of seriousness; bad faith; anguish; freedom as source of values; solitary drunkard and leader of nations

# Søren Kierkegaard — Section Guide

Section-by-section summaries of Kierkegaard's major works, for the "Show
sources" panel. Each entry names one titled section of one work and says, in
our own words, what that section argues.

**Source method:** Summaries are written fresh from the Danish critical
edition, *Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter* (SKS, Søren Kierkegaard Research
Centre; online text via the Royal Danish Library, dedicated to the public
domain under CC0). Chapter-level English titles follow the Hong (Princeton,
*Kierkegaard's Writings*) edition's published contents; sub-section titles are
the SKS Danish headings rendered in Hong's terminology (e.g. "anxiety", not
"dread"; "follower", not "disciple"). SEP and IEP are secondary references
only and are link-only per `data/rag/source_manifest.json`. No verbatim
copying from any English translation; quoted phrases are limited to standard
English renderings of Kierkegaard's own coinages, each given with the Danish
it renders.

**Pseudonyms:** most of these works are pseudonymous, and Kierkegaard
insisted the pseudonyms' views are not simply his own. Each summary names the
speaker the work names: *Fear and Trembling* — Johannes de silentio;
*Philosophical Fragments* — Johannes Climacus; *The Concept of Anxiety* —
Vigilius Haufniensis; *The Sickness unto Death* — Anti-Climacus. *Works of
Love* is signed by Kierkegaard himself.

**Locators:** part, chapter and section *titles and numbers* only, never page
numbers, because pagination differs across editions and translations. Where a
section begins with untitled opening remarks of a larger heading (e.g. the
lead-in to *The Sickness unto Death*, Part One, C), those remarks are included
in the first titled sub-section after them.

## Fear and Trembling (1843)

### Fear and Trembling — Preface

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Preface

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio charges his age with a clearance sale of ideas: every student of philosophy claims to have doubted everything and to "go further" (gaae videre), without saying how. Descartes, by contrast, never doubted with respect to faith and offered his method as valid only for himself; the ancient Greeks treated doubt as a lifelong task. Faith too was once the work of a lifetime, in which even the tested old man kept his youthful fear and trembling; now everyone assumes it and passes beyond. He says he is no philosopher and has not understood the System, and argues that recasting faith's whole content as concepts would not mean grasping faith or how one entered it. He calls himself a supplementary clerk writing as a luxury, expecting to be ignored or chopped into paragraphs.

**Key terms:** going further; doubting everything; Descartes; the System; faith as a lifelong task; Johannes de silentio

### Fear and Trembling — Exordium

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Exordium

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio tells of a man, neither thinker nor Hebrew scholar, who understood the story of God testing Abraham less the older he grew, and longed to have ridden the three-day journey to Mount Moriah. Four ways he pictured it follow. In the first, Abraham feigns idolatrous desire so that Isaac will think him a monster rather than lose faith in God. In the second, he sacrifices the ram in silence but can never forget that God demanded this, and sees no more joy. In the third, he begs forgiveness for having been willing, yet cannot see how that was sin, or if sin, how forgivable. In the fourth, Isaac sees Abraham's hand clench in despair and loses his faith. Each is paired with an image of a mother weaning her child.

**Key terms:** Mount Moriah; the three-day journey; the four versions of Abraham; weaning; Isaac losing faith; the man who longed to understand Abraham

**References:**
- Primary text: Fear and Trembling, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Exordium; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition

### Fear and Trembling — Eulogy on Abraham

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Eulogy on Abraham

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio argues that without an eternal consciousness life would be despair, and that beside the hero God made the poet, who can only admire him and keep his memory alive. Each person is great in proportion to what he loved, expected, and strove with; whoever loved God, expected the impossible, and strove with God is greatest, and Abraham greatest of all. Abraham kept believing the promise of a son after it had become unreasonable; had he resigned the wish he would have been remembered, but not as the father of faith, since holding fast to the temporal after giving it up is greater. When God demands Isaac, Abraham believes for this life, not a life to come; had he doubted, he might have plunged the knife into his own breast, admired but no guiding star for the anxious; or, had he found the ram only by chance, his return home would have been a flight. In 130 years he got no further than faith.

**Key terms:** father of faith; the poet and the hero; expecting the impossible; strove with God; faith for this life; Mount Moriah

### Fear and Trembling — Problemata: Preliminary Expectoration

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Problemata, "Preliminary Expectoration"

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio argues that in the world of spirit only the one who works gets the bread, and that preachers who praise Abraham for offering his best leave out the anxiety: ethically he meant to murder Isaac, religiously to sacrifice him. He imagines a pastor thundering at a sleepless listener who goes home to imitate Abraham. He can follow Hegel, but Abraham paralyzes his thought; he can make the movement of resignation but not of faith. He contrasts the "knight of infinite resignation" (Ridderen af den uendelige Resignation), a youth who gives up a princess he can never have and is reconciled in pain, with the "knight of faith" (Troens Ridder), who makes the same renunciation and then believes he will get her "by virtue of the absurd" (i Kraft af det Absurde), since for God all things are possible. Resignation is a human movement that wins one's eternal consciousness; faith regains finitude, so Abraham received Isaac rather than giving him up. Never having found one, he imagines a knight of faith who looks just like a tax collector. The section announces the Problemata, to draw out the paradox that can make a murder holy, where thought stops and faith begins.

**Key terms:** infinite resignation; knight of faith; the absurd; double movement; the youth and the princess; the knight who looks like a tax collector; anxiety

### Fear and Trembling — Problema I: Is There a Teleological Suspension of the Ethical?

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Problema I "Is There a Teleological Suspension of the Ethical?"

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio grants that the ethical is the universal, valid for everyone at every moment, and that the single individual's task is to annul his particularity in it; if that is the highest, Hegel is right, but then he should condemn Abraham as a murderer rather than let him be honored as father of faith. Faith is the paradox that the individual, having been in the universal, stands higher than it in an absolute relation to the absolute, beyond all mediation. The "tragic hero" (tragisk Helt), Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus, stays within the ethical by sacrificing one ethical duty to a higher ethical goal, and so can be understood and wept over. Abraham serves no nation or state; his act is private, for God's sake and his own, and his temptation is the ethical itself. So either there is a "teleological suspension of the ethical" (teleologisk Suspension af det Ethiske) or Abraham is lost. He cannot speak or be judged by the outcome, against the lecturers who judge great men by results; thoughtless talk likewise strips the anxiety and paradox from the Virgin Mary.

**Key terms:** teleological suspension of the ethical; the ethical as the universal; the single individual; tragic hero; Agamemnon, Jephthah, Brutus; mediation; judging by the outcome

### Fear and Trembling — Problema II: Is There an Absolute Duty to God?

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Problema II "Is There an Absolute Duty to God?"

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio argues that if the ethical is the divine, every duty is a duty to God only by reference, and God shrinks to an invisible vanishing point; then Hegel, who ranks the outer above the inner, is right, but may not call Abraham the father of faith. Faith is a new inwardness incommensurable with any outward expression, not the immediacy that recent philosophy substitutes for it; Socrates' ignorance is only infinite resignation. If there is an "absolute duty to God" (absolut Pligt mod Gud), the ethical becomes relative: the individual fixes his relation to the universal through his relation to the absolute. He reads Luke 14:26 on hating father and mother literally, against an exegete who softens it, while insisting that Abraham must love Isaac with his whole soul, for only then is the act a sacrifice, though ethically it remains murder. Unlike the tragic hero, the knight of faith cannot make himself understood, cannot be helped by another knight, and stays sleepless with no rest in the universal, yet addresses God as Thou. The true knight is absolutely isolated and a witness, never a teacher; the counterfeit seeks company among sectarians.

**Key terms:** absolute duty to God; inwardness and outwardness; Luke 14:26; hating father and mother; knight of faith as witness, not teacher; sectarians; the tragic hero

### Fear and Trembling — Problema III: Was It Ethically Defensible for Abraham to Conceal His Undertaking from Sarah, from Eliezer, and from Isaac?

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Problema III "Was It Ethically Defensible for Abraham to Conceal His Undertaking from Sarah, from Eliezer, and from Isaac?"

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio argues that the ethical, as the universal, is the disclosed, so the individual's ethical task is to leave hiddenness; Hegel allows no justified concealment, and so may not call Abraham the father of faith. Taking up "the interesting" (det Interessante) as the border between aesthetics and ethics, he shows that aesthetics rewards silence meant to spare another while ethics demands disclosure: Agamemnon should tell Iphigenia himself. Silence is both demonic and divine. He tests a series of figures: the Delphi bridegroom warned by augurs, who could speak because an augur's word is public; the merman who spares Agnes and can either stay hidden in repentance (becoming demonic, or finding peace only by being lost to the world) or be disclosed and saved through Agnes, after which belonging to her takes a further movement by virtue of the absurd; Sarah in the Book of Tobit, the true heroine; Gloucester, made demonic by being unable to bear pity; a sympathetic Faust who hides his doubt. Abraham passes over Sarah, Eliezer, and Isaac; he is neither aesthetic nor tragic hero, and cannot speak because no one could understand him. His one reply to Isaac, that God will see to the lamb, has the form of irony: it says nothing yet is no lie, and expresses the double movement of resignation and faith. Socrates, an "intellectual tragic hero" (intellectuel tragisk Helt), needed a last word a poet could have invented; no poet reaches Abraham's.

**Key terms:** concealment and disclosure; the interesting; silence; Agnes and the merman; Sarah in Tobit; Faust; the demonic; Abraham's reply to Isaac; irony

**References:**
- Primary text: Fear and Trembling, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Problema III; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition
- IEP, "Kierkegaard, Søren", §2b "Fear and Trembling and Repetition" — https://iep.utm.edu/kierkega/

### Fear and Trembling — Epilogue

**Work:** Fear and Trembling (1843, pseud. Johannes de silentio)

**Locator:** Epilogue

**Summary:** Johannes de silentio opens with Dutch spice merchants who sank cargoes to raise prices, and asks whether the age needs a similar deception about spirit; he argues it needs instead an honest earnestness that keeps the tasks young, difficult, and inviting. What is genuinely human, passion, no generation learns from the one before; each begins at the beginning, as with love. Faith is the highest passion, and no generation gets further in it than the last, though perhaps many in each generation never reach it (whether many in the present age fail to, he leaves undecided); one who does reach it neither stalls nor goes further, since his life is in it. Children who exhaust every game before noon show not progress but a lack of the earnestness that play requires. He ends with Heraclitus, who said one cannot step into the same river twice, and the disciple who went further, saying not even once, and thereby turned the teaching into an Eleatic denial of motion.

**Key terms:** going further; faith as the highest passion; each generation begins anew; Heraclitus and his disciple; the Dutch spice merchants

**References:**
- Primary text: Fear and Trembling, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Epilogue; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition

## Philosophical Fragments (1844)

### Philosophical Fragments — Preface

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Preface

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus presents the book as a mere pamphlet, written on his own account and with no claim to a place in the scholarly enterprise of the System. He calls himself an idler from indolence, yet likens himself to Diogenes rolling his tub through Corinth while the city prepared for Philip's siege, so as not to be the only idle one. The danger he fears most is that someone will credit the pamphlet with world-historical importance, and he mocks the age's endless shouting of era, epoch, and System. He refuses to say what his opinion is: having one is both too much and too little for someone who wants to dance lightly in the service of thought, and he can stake only his own life, never another's. A closing proposition says the question is asked by one who is ignorant and does not know what prompts it.

**Key terms:** pamphlet; the System; world-historical significance; Diogenes; having an opinion; the thought of death

**References:**
- Primary text: Philosophical Fragments, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Preface; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. I, A: Thought-Project

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter I "Thought-Project", A

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus asks whether the truth can be learned and sets out the Socratic answer. Against the Meno's dilemma, that one can seek neither what one knows nor what one does not know, Socrates holds that all learning is recollection: the truth was already in the learner, which yields a retrogressive proof of the soul's pre-existence. Socrates therefore remained a midwife, forbidden by the god to give birth, because between one human being and another that is the highest relation. Socratically, any point of departure in time is merely an "occasion" (Anledning), and so is the teacher; whether I learned from Socrates, Prodicus, or a housemaid can concern me only historically, never with respect to my eternal happiness. The moment of discovery vanishes at once into the eternal. Climacus contrasts this with an age in which every other person is an authority.

**Key terms:** can the truth be learned; recollection; Socrates as midwife; occasion; point of departure in time; eternal happiness; Meno

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. I, B: Thought-Project (The Preceding State; The Teacher; The Follower)

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter I "Thought-Project", B (a "The Preceding State", b "The Teacher", c "The Follower")

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus builds the alternative: suppose the moment in time has decisive significance. Then the learner cannot possess the truth even in ignorance; he is untruth, and a human teacher can only occasion his discovering that. To receive the truth he must also be given the condition for understanding it, which only the god can supply. Having once had that condition, he must have forfeited it by his own fault, which Climacus calls sin, and he cannot free himself (a note pictures a child who spent his coin on a toy and cannot trade it for the book). The teacher who restores the condition is Savior, Deliverer, Reconciler, and Judge; the moment is the "fullness of time" (Tidens Fylde); and the follower becomes a new person through conversion, repentance, and "rebirth" (Gjenfødelsen). Greek pathos centres on recollection, this project's on the moment. To a critic who mocks the project, Climacus answers that, unlike gunpowder, no human being invented it.

**Key terms:** the moment; untruth; the condition; sin; Savior; fullness of time; conversion; rebirth

- Primary text: Philosophical Fragments, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Ch. I B; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. II: The God as Teacher and Savior (A Poetical Venture)

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter II "The God as Teacher and Savior (A Poetical Venture)"

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus contrasts Socrates, whose teaching was a reciprocal relation of occasion, with the god, who needs no follower and can be moved to appear only by love. Love aims to win the learner, and understanding requires equality, so the god's love is unhappy: the lovers cannot understand each other. Climacus illustrates with a king who loves a lowly maiden and fears she could never forget that he was king. Unity by ascent, lifting the learner up or dazzling him with glory, would deceive. Unity must come by descent: the god appears in "the form of a servant" (Tjenerens Skikkelse), not as a disguise like the king's cloak but as his true form, so that his whole life is suffering, ending in death. To a critic who calls this plagiarism, Climacus replies that no human being, and not the race either, could have composed it; it is not a poem but "the wonder" (Vidunderet).

**Key terms:** the god as teacher; unhappy love; the king and the lowly maiden; the form of a servant; ascent and descent; equality; the wonder

**References:**

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. III: The Absolute Paradox (A Metaphysical Caprice)

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter III "The Absolute Paradox (A Metaphysical Caprice)"

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus starts from Socrates, who could not tell whether he was a monster stranger than Typhon or a gentler creature sharing in the divine. The paradox is the "passion of thought" (Tankens Lidenskab): the understanding's highest passion is to will a collision that will be its downfall, to discover what it cannot think. What it collides with is "the unknown" (det Ubekjendte), which he names the god. Proving its existence is folly, since one reasons from existence, never to it: Napoleon's deeds do not prove Napoleon, and existence emerges from a proof only by a leap, as when one lets go of a Cartesian doll. The unknown is the absolutely different, yet the understanding cannot think absolute difference and ends by confusing it with likeness. Only the god can teach that the difference is sin, so the paradox both exposes that difference and wills to annul it in equality; the passion in which understanding and paradox meet is not yet named.

**Key terms:** the absolute paradox; the understanding; the unknown; proofs of God's existence; the leap; the absolutely different; consciousness of sin

### Philosophical Fragments — Appendix: Offense at the Paradox (An Acoustical Illusion)

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Appendix to Chapter III "Offense at the Paradox (An Acoustical Illusion)"

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus argues that when the understanding and the paradox fail to meet in mutual understanding, the relation is unhappy: an unhappy love of the understanding, if he may put it so, though the analogy goes no further, which he proposes to call, more precisely, "offense" (Forargelse). All offense is at bottom a suffering, even when it shows itself as defiant mockery; suffering and active offense each contain something of the other. Offense is not the understanding's own discovery but an "acoustical illusion" (akustisk Bedrag): it seems to come from elsewhere while merely echoing the paradox. When offense calls the moment and the paradox folly, it is sounding back the paradox's own claim that the understanding is the absurd; when it calls the paradox absurd and improbable, it repeats what the paradox said of itself first. If the paradox, like truth, is the criterion both of itself and of what is false, offense can be regarded as an indirect test of whether the paradox is right; at root it is a misunderstanding of the moment. An imagined critic objects that these sayings belong to Tertullian, Hamann, Luther, and others; Climacus agrees, noting that these speakers held fast to the paradox yet sounded offended.

**Key terms:** offense; acoustical illusion; the absurd; the moment; suffering and active offense; the understanding

- Primary text: Philosophical Fragments, Søren Kierkegaards Skrifter (SKS), Appendix to Ch. III; English titles follow the Hong (Princeton) edition

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. IV: The Situation of the Contemporary Follower

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter IV "The Situation of the Contemporary Follower"

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus continues his poem: the god's servant form is real, not a disguise he could throw off like the king. The curious crowd around the teacher, and the scholar who comes secretly to test him, are not followers. Climacus now names the happy passion in which the understanding steps aside and the paradox gives itself: "faith" (Tro), which is itself the condition the god gives. Its object is the teacher, not the teaching, and it is neither knowledge, since no knowing can take as its object the absurdity that the eternal is historical, nor an act of will. Closeness makes no one a follower, not even a contemporary who kept a hundred spies on the teacher; the god is not directly recognizable. To an interlocutor asking whether contemporaries had an advantage, Climacus answers that direct contemporaneity is only an occasion: whoever receives the condition from the god, later believers included, is the real contemporary, in the "autopsy of faith" (Troens Autopsi).

### Philosophical Fragments — Ch. V: The Follower at Second Hand

**Work:** Philosophical Fragments (1844, pseud. Johannes Climacus)

**Locator:** Chapter V "The Follower at Second Hand"

**Summary:** Johannes Climacus asks whether those living 1,843 years after the teacher stand differently from his contemporaries, and argues that the question is wrongly posed. Comparing the first generation of secondary followers with the last, he finds each supposed advantage double-edged. The first is nearer to reliable reports, but a tyrant who starved the surviving witnesses to extract a perfectly consistent account would only be deceived; its real advantage is nearness to the shock, which makes one attentive, whether to offense or to faith. ### The Concept of Anxiety — Introduction

**Work:** The Concept of Anxiety (1844, pseud. Vigilius Haufniensis)

**Locator:** Introduction

**Summary:** Vigilius Haufniensis argues that every problem has its proper place among the sciences, and that current thinking blurs these places: logic's last section is titled Actuality, dogmatics calls faith the immediate, speculative knowing is called reconciliation, and Hegel's philosophy makes the negative both the motor of logic and, in ethics, evil. His book treats anxiety psychologically with the dogma of hereditary sin in view, but sin belongs to no science; handled aesthetically, metaphysically, or psychologically, its concept and its proper mood, earnestness, are falsified, and psychology turns it into a state. ### The Concept of Anxiety — Ch. IV, §1: Anxiety about Evil

### The Concept of Anxiety — Ch. IV, §2: Anxiety about the Good (The Demonic)

**Work:** The Concept of Anxiety (1844, pseud. Vigilius Haufniensis)

**Locator:** Chapter IV, §2 "Anxiety about the Good (The Demonic)"

Freedom may be lost somatically-psychically, down to animal perdition, or pneumatically, as a lack of certitude and inwardness that appears in paired forms: the prover of immortality who is not convinced and, opposite him, the rigid orthodox; the orthodox and the freethinker; unbelief (whose highest expression is scoffing) and superstition; hypocrisy and offense; pride and cowardice. Inwardness is "earnestness" (Alvor), which returns with the same originality each Sunday for a pastor, and whose proper object is oneself; Macbeth shows its loss. ### Works of Love — First Series, I: Love's Hidden Life and Its Recognizability by Its Fruits

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

### Works of Love — First Series, V: Our Duty to Remain in Love's Debt to One Another

### Works of Love — Second Series, I: Love Builds Up

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

**Locator:** Second Series, I "Love Builds Up"

**Summary:** Kierkegaard argues that only love builds up: to build up is to raise something from its foundation, and spiritually love is that foundation, so love is at once ground and building and what it builds up is love. Because all speech about the spirit uses ordinary words in a carried-over sense, he first examines the plain word: adding a wing or ten more ells of height is not building up, while raising even a low house from the ground is (Luke 6:48). Anything becomes upbuilding exactly where love is present, as with a mother watching her sleeping child or an artist who smashes his masterpiece out of love for someone. Since only God can plant love in a heart, the one who loves builds up by presupposing that love is present at the ground in the other, compelling himself rather than the other; unlike the master builder or teacher, he has nothing to show and claims no merit. He reads Paul's attributes of love in 1 Corinthians 13 as so many ways of presupposing love, illustrated by the prodigal son's father. Love, unlike wisdom, is not a quality one has for oneself but one by which one exists for others. The range opens with the Second Series' brief preface.

### Works of Love — Second Series, II: Love Believes All Things—and Yet Is Never Deceived

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

**Locator:** Second Series, II "Love Believes All Things—and Yet Is Never Deceived"

**Summary:** Kierkegaard argues that love believes all things not out of ignorance, as light-mindedness and inexperience do, but by a choice, and that this very choice secures it against every deception. Mistrust and love share the same knowledge, which at its height only holds opposite possibilities in equilibrium, since deception reaches exactly as far as truth; he quotes a long reflection on hidden inwardness that the most mistrustful and the most loving person could equally have spoken. Decision comes only in the ergo of belief, so mistrust passes off its unbelief as an inference from knowledge, while love draws the opposite conclusion; whoever judges another thereby discloses himself. Against the common one-sided fear of thinking too well of people he sets the simple wise man of antiquity who feared error above all. For true love the only possible deception is self-deception: as a child cannot really deceive its parents, the true lover, who demands no love in return, cannot be cheated, whereas the infatuated, self-loving person who can love only one can. A deceiver who wins the true lover's love merely enriches the lover and deceives himself, like the man lying in the ditch who thinks he is riding.

### Works of Love — Second Series, III: Love Hopes All Things—and Yet Is Never Put to Shame

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

**Locator:** Second Series, III "Love Hopes All Things—and Yet Is Never Put to Shame"

**Summary:** Kierkegaard argues that love, being greater than hope, takes on hope's work as a task: hoping all things for others, and always. Christianity opens temporal life to the eternal; hoping all things is the task in the form of eternity, always hoping the same task in the form of time. Since the eternal touches time only as the possible, which is always double, relating expectantly to the possibility of the good is hope and to that of evil is fear, and choosing hope is an eternal decision. He rejects assigning hope to youth as a stage of life: whoever lives without possibility is in despair, and eternity brings a person up through possibility as one sets a child a great task a little at a time. Because love is the "middle term" (Mellembestemmelsen), one hopes for oneself exactly as much as for others. Shame lies not in disappointed hope but in what one hoped; the father who hoped to the last for the prodigal son would stand in honor in eternity even were the son lost.

### Works of Love — Second Series, IV: Love Does Not Seek Its Own

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

**Locator:** Second Series, IV "Love Does Not Seek Its Own"

**Summary:** Kierkegaard argues that love does not seek its own because in love there is no mine and yours. God and Christ seek their own only by giving themselves, whereas a person who tries to become another's object of love does seek his own. Justice guards mine and yours and despairs when catastrophe confuses them; love is a blessed upheaval of that distinction. Erotic love and friendship merely exchange mine and yours into a shared ours, as in the exchange of rings, and so remain heightened self-love; the criminal abolishes yours and thereby loses his mine, while self-denying love abolishes mine and, owning nothing, gains God and everything. True love loves each person according to his distinctiveness, as nature loves the flowers of the field, unlike the domineering, who recasts others in his own image, and pettiness, which never dared to be itself before God. Finally, love gives so that the gift seems the recipient's own: the greatest benefaction, helping another stand alone, requires the helper to hide himself. The noble rogue of antiquity, the midwife (Socrates, though unnamed), hides his help behind the "dash" (Tankestreg) with a smile; for the one who loves, the dash conceals anxiety, sleepless labor and fear and trembling, and he takes no self-satisfaction in it, knowing that in the end everyone stands alone by God's help.

### Works of Love — Second Series, V: Love Hides a Multitude of Sins

**Work:** Works of Love (1847)

**Locator:** Second Series, V "Love Hides a Multitude of Sins"

Imagination, the medium of infinitizing (Fichte made it the origin of the categories), can make feeling, knowing or willing fantastic: fantastic feeling makes the self evaporate into an abstract sentimentality that belongs to no actual person, a sympathy with abstract humanity; knowledge without self-knowledge squanders the self, like lives spent building pyramids or playing one note in a Russian horn band; the will should become as concrete as it is abstract. A religious person intoxicated with existing before God says a sparrow's living is understandable, since it does not know it exists before God, but to know that one does and not go mad is beyond him. Anyone who has become fantastic in this way may still marry and be honored while losing a self, a loss that can pass as quietly as if it were nothing, perhaps without anyone noticing.

### The Sickness unto Death — Pt. 2, B.A: The Sin of Despairing over One's Sin

**Work:** The Sickness unto Death (1849, pseud. Anti-Climacus)

**Locator:** Part Two, B, A "The Sin of Despairing over One's Sin"

**Summary:** Anti-Climacus argues that to despair over one's own sin is not one more sin, like stealing a thousand after a hundred, but the state of sin intensified in a new consciousness. Sin is a break with the good; despair over sin is a second break, with repentance: sin shuts itself in, cuts the bridge behind it, and treats repentance and grace as enemies, which is why Mephistopheles rightly says nothing is more wretched than a devil who despairs, despairing here meaning being weak enough to listen to talk of repentance and grace. Like a balloonist rising by dropping ballast, the despairer sinks while feeling lighter, throwing off the good. Macbeth, after the murder, loses both grace and himself. The section then warns that this despair often passes for depth: a man who relapses after long resistance vows never to forgive himself, or says God never can, while a foolish pastor is perhaps not far from admiring his deep soul and his own wife feels humbled beside so holy a man; it is wounded pride, turned away from God, when he should humbly thank God for the help he had.

**Key terms:** despair over sin; break with repentance; the demonic; Mephistopheles; balloon image; relapse; never forgiving oneself

### The Sickness unto Death — Pt. 2, B.B: The Sin of Despairing of the Forgiveness of Sins (Offense)

**Work:** The Sickness unto Death (1849, pseud. Anti-Climacus)

**Locator:** Part Two, B, B "The Sin of Despairing of the Forgiveness of Sins (Offense)"

**Summary:** Anti-Climacus argues that the self is intensified once more as a self before Christ: God's being born, suffering, and dying for this self makes Christ its measure, and as the self grows, its sin grows more intense. Here weakness and defiance trade places: refusing to be oneself as a sinner, and so doing without forgiveness, is defiance; willing to be oneself as a sinner beyond forgiveness is weakness. Saying no to forgiveness looks like closing in on God, yet one must be farthest from God to say it. Since the ethical "You shall" (Du skal) was dropped, such despair is praised as a sign of depth, and God has become an over-familiar figure the pastor thanks people for visiting. This despair is offense: the Jews were right to be offended that Christ would forgive sins. Christendom's misfortune is that the teaching about the God-man, preached incessantly, has been taken in vain, and the qualitative difference has been pantheistically abolished, until the crowd itself is made God. Christianity guards against this with the doctrine of sin, which speculation cannot think because sin belongs to the single individual. Sin can be said of God by neither negation nor eminence, and in forgiving sins God is most unlike us. Judgment is always of individuals: a crowd escapes punishment for mutiny, but before God each is transparent, like the fleeing murderer whose description races ahead by telegraph.

**Key terms:** despair of forgiveness; offense; self before Christ; the God-man; qualitative difference; the single individual; the crowd; judgment and conscience

### The Sickness unto Death — Pt. 2, B.C: The Sin of Dismissing Christianity Modo Ponendo

**Work:** The Sickness unto Death (1849, pseud. Anti-Climacus)

**Locator:** Part Two, B, C "The Sin of Dismissing Christianity Modo Ponendo, of Declaring It to Be Untruth"

**Summary:** Anti-Climacus calls declaring Christianity a lie the "sin against the Holy Spirit" (Synd mod den Hellig-Aand), the self's most despairing intensification. Picturing sin as a war with God, he traces a change of tactics: despair and despair over sin are defensive, despair of forgiveness takes a position, and this sin goes on the offensive. The possibility of offense is God's safeguard against human beings coming too near: between God and human being lies an "infinite qualitative difference" (uendelig Qvalitets-Forskjel), and even the God-man cannot remove offense, the sorrow in Christ's love; hence his warning that the one not offended at him is blessed. A human lover who sees his sacrifice might ruin the beloved either shrinks back or acts in tears. He ranks offense's forms: leaving Christ undecided, which denies the "You shall" (Du skal) (like an official ignoring a visiting king); the negative, suffering form that cannot stop staring at the paradox; and the positive form, denying Christ docetically or rationalistically, which makes him the devil's invention. The book closes by recalling that its formula for the self free of despair is the definition of faith.

**Key terms:** sin against the Holy Spirit; offense; infinite qualitative difference; possibility of offense; forms of offense; docetism and rationalism; faith
